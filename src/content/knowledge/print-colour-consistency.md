Buyers often expect the second print run to match the first exactly. It rarely does, and understanding why separates a normal variation from a supplier problem.

## What varies

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Ink batch | Small hue shifts between ink lots |
| Substrate | Same ink on coated vs uncoated reads differently |
| Press conditions | Temperature, speed, anilox volume |
| Digital vs flexo | Different colour engines entirely |
| Laminate | Gloss vs matte changes perceived colour |
| Light | The same label looks different under shop lighting |

A visible-but-small variation between runs is normal in print. A large shift usually means the substrate or the print method changed — not that the printer was careless.

## How much is normal

In flexo and digital, a Delta E of around 2–3 is generally imperceptible to most viewers; above 4–5 it becomes noticeable side by side. Without a measured reference, "match the previous run" is an opinion rather than a specification.

The practical approach: approve a physical reference sample, keep it, and compare future runs against it rather than against memory.

## When to specify a spot colour

A spot colour is a pre-mixed ink matched to a reference (Pantone or a physical swatch). It is the only way to get an exact, repeatable brand colour across runs and substrates.

Cost: an extra plate in flexo, or a dedicated ink in digital — and a setup charge. Worth it for a brand colour on packaging; rarely worth it for a one-off sticker run.

## Proofs

- **Digital proof** — a PDF or screen preview; good for layout, useless for colour.
- **Contract proof** — a calibrated inkjet proof on the actual stock; a reasonable colour reference.
- **Press proof** — a short run on the production press; the most accurate, and the most expensive.

For brand colours, a contract proof or a physical sample is the minimum. For everything else, approving on screen is acceptable if the design is not colour-critical.

## What this means for buyers

**Keep the approved sample.** It is the only objective reference for the next run.

**Do not expect exact matches across substrates.** The same ink on paper and film looks different; that is physics, not error.

**Spot colour is a budget decision.** Exact brand matching costs a plate; decide whether the match matters enough.

**Ask what proof you are approving.** "Proof" covers three very different things.
