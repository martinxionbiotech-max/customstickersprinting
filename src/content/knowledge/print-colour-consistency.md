Buyers often expect the second print run to match the first exactly. It rarely does, and understanding why separates a normal variation from a supplier problem.

## What varies

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Ink batch | Small hue shifts between ink lots |
| Substrate | Same ink on coated vs uncoated reads differently |
| Press conditions | Temperature, speed, anilox volume |
| Digital vs flexo | Different colour engines entirely |
| Laminate | Gloss vs matte changes perceived colour |
| Light | The same label looks different under shop lighting |

A visible-but-small variation between runs is normal in print. A large shift usually means the substrate or the print method changed — not that the printer was careless.

## How much is normal

In flexo and digital, a Delta E of around 2–3 is generally imperceptible to most viewers; above 4–5 it becomes noticeable side by side. Without a measured reference, "match the previous run" is an opinion rather than a specification.

The practical approach: approve a physical reference sample, keep it, and compare future runs against it rather than against memory.

## When to specify a spot colour

A spot colour is a pre-mixed ink matched to a reference (Pantone or a physical swatch). It is the only way to get an exact, repeatable brand colour across runs and substrates.

Cost: an extra plate in flexo, or a dedicated ink in digital — and a setup charge. Worth it for a brand colour on packaging; rarely worth it for a one-off sticker run.

## Proofs

- **Digital proof** — a PDF or screen preview; good for layout, useless for colour.
- **Contract proof** — a calibrated inkjet proof on the actual stock; a reasonable colour reference.
- **Press proof** — a short run on the production press; the most accurate, and the most expensive.

For brand colours, a contract proof or a physical sample is the minimum. For everything else, approving on screen is acceptable if the design is not colour-critical.

## A colour-control routine for repeat orders

A repeat order is a comparison, not a fresh design. Keep the approved physical sample, write the material and print method on it, and store it away from light. When the next run arrives, compare it under the same light and at the same angle. A difference that is invisible on a screen can be obvious side by side.

For a brand colour, decide the tolerance before the first run. A Delta E of about 2–3 is generally imperceptible; 4–5 becomes visible side by side. If the colour is a brand asset, a spot colour and a retained reference are more reliable than another CMYK conversion.

Ask three questions before approving a repeat: is the substrate the same, is the print method the same, and is the laminate the same? A change in any of them changes the colour. A matte laminate can make the same ink look darker and flatter than a gloss laminate; a coated stock can make it look brighter than an uncoated one.

If the colour is critical, budget for a contract proof or a press proof. A PDF proof is a layout tool, not a colour reference.

## What this means for buyers

**Keep the approved sample.** It is the only objective reference for the next run.

**Do not expect exact matches across substrates.** The same ink on paper and film looks different; that is physics, not error.

**Spot colour is a budget decision.** Exact brand matching costs a plate; decide whether the match matters enough.

**Ask what proof you are approving.** "Proof" covers three very different things.

## Separating an acceptable ink drift from a changed specification

A colour complaint often begins with a comparison that is not controlled. The previous label may have been kept on a sunny desk, while the new one is viewed under an office lamp. One may be glossy and the other matte, or the two runs may use different stock. Memory and screens widen the apparent gap. The run may be within normal variation while the reference has changed, or the supplier may have changed a specification without saying so.

A fair comparison depends on the standard and the evidence behind it. A spot brand colour deserves a retained physical reference and an agreed tolerance; a photographic label may need only approval on a contract proof. If the substrate, print method or laminate changes, an exact match is not the right demand. Lighting should stay constant, and a sample stored away from daylight is more useful than one displayed as a showpiece.

The comparison should record what was printed on, how it was finished, and which sample is the master. Keep that reference protected and mark it with the full specification. Compare a new run under the same lamp and look at both copies directly rather than one after the other. | Bench comparison | Hidden variable | More defensible test |
|---|---|---|
| New label against memory | Time and viewing conditions have changed | Use a labelled physical master |
| New label against a screen | Emitted light exaggerates saturation | Compare two printed copies |
| Matte sample against gloss sample | The laminate changes perceived colour | Hold sheen constant first |
| Old master kept on a shelf | Light may have faded the reference | Store the approved copy dark |
| Two stocks, same ink recipe | Ink sits differently on each surface | Match stock before judging colour |
