Quantity is the only variable in a label order that a buyer controls completely, and it is usually decided by the price break rather than by production reality. That is how brands end up with 2,000 labels for a product they discontinued.

This page gives a quantity formula, the spoilage and shelf-life numbers behind it, and the cases where the price break is worth taking.

Part of the [cost and ordering guide](/knowledge/custom-label-printing-cost/).

## The formula

```
order quantity = (units per production run × runs per order) + spoilage + samples
```

| Input | Typical value | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Units per run | Your batch size | Production plan |
| Runs per order | 1–3 | How often you reorder |
| Spoilage | 3–5% | Application errors, damaged labels, test units |
| Samples | 10–20 | Retailers, photographers, sales |

A 500-unit run, ordered for two runs, with 4% spoilage and 15 samples: 1,000 + 40 + 15 = 1,055 labels.

Round to the nearest quantity break if it is close — see below.

## Spoilage is not optional

Labels get damaged in three ways, and all three are normal:

- **Application errors** — misalignment, bubbles, a creased label on a curved bottle. Hand application typically wastes 2–4%.
- **Damaged during filling** — a wet or oily surface, a label applied over a drip.
- **Test and sample units** — photography, a retailer's sample request, a trade show.

Ordering exactly the production quantity guarantees a shortfall. Ordering 3–5% over costs a few percent more and prevents a panicked short run at a much worse unit price.

## The price break trade-off

Price breaks are real, but they are not free money. Compare the saving against the risk:

| Situation | Order | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Proven design, stable product | To the next break | Setup amortises, storage is manageable |
| New design, first run | One cycle + spoilage | A redesign makes stock worthless |
| Seasonal variant | One season | Next year's artwork will differ |
| Regulated text likely to change | One cycle | A regulation change strands inventory |
| Fast-growing product | Two cycles | Reordering mid-growth risks a stockout |

The break usually sits at 250, 500, 1,000, 2,500 and 5,000. Rounding up from 900 to 1,000 is often free; jumping from 500 to 5,000 is a different decision entirely.

## Shelf life and storage

Labels are not indefinitely stable:

- **Adhesive** — acrylic adhesives keep their tack for about 12 months in good conditions; some grades longer.
- **Laminate** — stable, but can yellow under UV.
- **Liner** — paper liners absorb moisture and curl, which jams applicators.

Storage rules:

| Condition | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Temperature | 15–25 °C |
| Humidity | 40–60% RH |
| Light | Out of direct sunlight |
| Position | Rolls upright, sheets flat, nothing on top |
| Environment | Away from solvents and food smells |

A roll stored in a hot warehouse for a summer can lose its differential release and refuse to unwind cleanly — a failure that looks like a manufacturing fault and is not.

## The cost of running out

A stockout is more expensive than the labels:

| Consequence | Cost |
|---|---|
| Emergency short run | Full setup again, at a worse unit price |
| Air freight instead of sea | Often more than the labels |
| Delayed launch | Marketing spend with nothing to sell |
| Retail penalty | Chargebacks for late or short delivery |

Against that, the cost of carrying an extra 10% of labels for a year is small — a few percent of the order value, plus storage. The asymmetry is why the spoilage allowance exists.

## A reorder trigger

Set one, rather than noticing when the box is empty:

```
reorder point = (weekly usage × lead time in weeks) + safety stock
```

For a product using 100 labels a week with a 3-week lead time and 200 labels of safety stock: reorder at 500 labels remaining.

That formula also explains why the production lead time matters commercially, not just operationally — see [production lead time](/knowledge/production-lead-time/).

## What this means for buyers

**Order one production cycle plus spoilage, not a year of stock.** The price break rarely compensates for dead inventory.

**Round to the break only when it is close.** 900 to 1,000 is free; 500 to 5,000 is a bet on the design.

**Store the stock properly.** Twelve months of shelf life assumes 15–25 °C and no direct sun.

**Set a reorder point.** Running out costs more than the discount you got by ordering exactly.

**Keep the die alive.** Reordering the same specification is the cheapest label you will ever buy.
