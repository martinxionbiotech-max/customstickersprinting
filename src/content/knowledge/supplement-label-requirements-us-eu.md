A supplement label has to do two jobs at once: it presents a product and it carries a structured declaration that tells the buyer what is in the product and how much. The declaration is not a marketing block. Its order, units, serving basis and wording come from the market's rules, and a change to one nutrient value can force the whole panel to be rebuilt.

This is general production information, not legal advice. The brand must confirm the final composition, claims and market route; see the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/). Part of the [label compliance guide](/knowledge/label-compliance/).

## What makes a dietary supplement different

In the United States, a dietary supplement is a category of food with its own statements and labelling rules under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act framework. In the EU, food supplements are governed by Directive 2002/46/EC, with additional national rules for permitted substances, maximum levels and labelling.

The category distinction matters before the design starts. A product marketed like a food may need a different panel from one marketed as a supplement. A product making a disease claim may not be a dietary supplement at all. The brand needs a regulatory determination, not a printer's opinion, before choosing the Supplement Facts or nutrient-panel layout.

## US: identity, net quantity and the firm

The US label normally needs a statement of identity, the net quantity of contents and the name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer or distributor. These are familiar retail fields, but they still compete for space with the Supplement Facts panel.

The identity should make clear what the product is, not only the brand name or flavour. The net quantity should use the required units and placement. The business address should be the entity information required by the rule, not a website alone. If a distributor is named, the qualifying statement should match the current requirement.

## US: the Supplement Facts panel

The Supplement Facts panel is the dominant layout element. It lists the serving size, the amount per serving and the nutrient or dietary ingredient sections in the prescribed order and units. The panel also carries the required footnote and format rules. 21 CFR 101.36 is the technical reference; the brand's regulatory adviser should confirm the current format for the product.

The numbers must come from the brand's formula and testing or calculation process. Do not ask a printer to adjust a nutrient value to make the panel tidier. If a value is missing, the panel is incomplete; if a serving size changes, the amount-per-serving column changes with it. A printer can reproduce the approved panel and flag that the type is too small, but cannot validate the formula.

## US: other ingredients and the disclaimer

Other ingredients are listed after the dietary ingredients, in the order the rule requires. The list may include capsule material, fillers, binders, flavours or preservatives. Ingredient names should be the approved names for the market, not a supplier's internal shorthand. The brand should check whether an ingredient is permitted and whether it needs a separate declaration.

When the label makes a structure/function claim, the required disclaimer must be displayed with the claim in the manner the FDA rules specify. The exact wording should be taken from the current FDA source. A statement that the product supports a normal body function is different from a statement that it treats a disease; the latter can change the product's regulatory category. The brand owns that decision, and the artwork should not be released until it is made.

## EU: Directive 2002/46/EC and national rules

Directive 2002/46/EC sets common EU requirements for food supplements. The label must identify the product, give the categories of nutrients or substances that characterise it, state the recommended portion per day, warn that the stated daily portion should not be exceeded, state that food supplements are not a substitute for a varied diet, and advise that they should be kept out of reach of young children.

The directive does not replace national rules for permitted vitamins, minerals, substances and levels. A product that is acceptable in one member state may need a different composition or additional national labelling in another. The importer or a qualified EU adviser should confirm the destination-country requirements and the language before the final text is approved.

## Mandatory fields in the US and EU

| Field | United States | European Union |
|---|---|---|
| Product identity | Statement of identity required | Product name and characterising nutrient or substance categories |
| Net quantity | Required net quantity | Required quantity, with the applicable national rules |
| Composition panel | Supplement Facts under 21 CFR 101.36 | Nutrient or substance declaration under the directive and national rules |
| Other ingredients | Required list where applicable | Ingredient information required by the applicable food rules |
| Responsible entity | Manufacturer, packer or distributor name and address | Food business operator details and any national importer details |
| Recommended portion | Serving size in the Supplement Facts panel | Recommended daily portion stated on the label |
| Warnings | Product-specific and claim-related statements | Not-exceed, varied-diet and keep-out-of-reach warnings, plus national additions |
| Claims | Structure/function claims trigger the FDA disclaimer | Separate health-claim and national rules apply |
| Language | English generally, with limited exceptions | Language of the market or a language easily understood there |

## Claims are the brand's responsibility

The label can carry a claim only if the brand can support it for the market. A nutrient-content statement, a structure/function statement, a health claim and a disease claim do not have the same regulatory consequences. The label wording, the substantiation file and the market route should be reviewed together.

| Claim type | What it may do to the label | Who must decide |
|---|---|---|
| Nutrient content | Describes the amount of a nutrient, often with a prescribed format | Brand and regulatory adviser confirm the permitted wording |
| Structure/function | Describes support for a normal body function and may trigger a disclaimer | Brand substantiates it and obtains market-specific review |
| Health claim | Links a substance to a health outcome under the market's rules | Brand confirms the authorised or notified route |
| Disease claim | Can change the product category or make the product a medicine | Brand must obtain regulatory advice before printing |
| Testimonial | Adds a claim-like message that still needs a basis | Brand owns the evidence and the required disclaimer |

## Artwork space and the handoff

Start with the Supplement Facts or nutrient panel at final size, then add the other-ingredients list, warnings, firm details and variable data. Keep the approved text in its own file with the market and version named. If the panel has a fixed border and type hierarchy, do not let a background pattern or low-contrast finish erode it.

Use the [label size calculator](/tools/label-size-calculator/) to compare a single panel with a wrap or a folded label. The [cosmetic label guide](/knowledge/cosmetic-label-requirements-us-eu/) shows a similar method for a different regulated category.

## Small packages and production control

A small bottle or sachet may force a choice between a fold-out label and a separate leaflet. The brand should confirm which construction the market permits and whether the panel can be read without tearing the pack. The printer can supply a physical dummy, but the dummy cannot decide whether the information presentation is acceptable for the destination.

Variable data adds another control point. A batch or lot code, a date and a market-specific overprint can share the same small area. Keep the variable fields separate from the fixed artwork, provide a data file with one row per printed unit, and approve a sample that shows the longest data string. If a code changes the position of a warning or pushes the panel beyond the safe area, the layout needs a fixed zone rather than a last-minute adjustment at the applicator.

## Questions to settle before print

1. Is the product a dietary supplement, a food or a medicine in each destination market?
2. Which ingredients, nutrient levels and claims are permitted there?
3. Which Supplement Facts or EU nutrient format has been approved?
4. Which warnings and disclaimers apply to the approved claims?
5. Which entity and language versions will appear on the final label?

The printer's responsibility begins with the approved content and ends with a faithful, legible reproduction. The brand's responsibility is to decide what that content should be. Keeping those decisions separate makes the print conversation shorter and the label easier to defend.
