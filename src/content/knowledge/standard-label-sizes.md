Buyers often ask for a "standard size" as though one existed. It does not: no standard governs label dimensions. What exists is a short list of sizes that are asked for so often that printers keep a die for them — and that is what makes them cheaper.

If one of these fits your container, use it. If none does, the custom size is a small part of the total cost.

## The common sizes

| Size (in) | Size (mm) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 × 3 | 25.4 × 76.2 | Neck labels, slim bottle panels |
| 1.5 × 1.5 | 38.1 × 38.1 | Small jar lids, sample seals |
| 2 × 2 | 50.8 × 50.8 | 4 oz jars, favours, logo stickers |
| 2 × 3 | 50.8 × 76.2 | Small bottles, lip balm multipacks |
| 2.5 × 3 | 63.5 × 76.2 | 8 oz jars, ingredient panels |
| 3 × 3 | 76.2 × 76.2 | 12 oz jars, candle tins |
| 3 × 4 | 76.2 × 101.6 | 250 ml bottles, pouches |
| 3.5 × 5 | 88.9 × 127 | 500 ml bottles, larger jars |
| 4 × 4 | 101.6 × 101.6 | 16 oz jars, carton faces |
| 4 × 6 | 101.6 × 152.4 | Shipping and carton labels |
| 5 × 7 | 127 × 177.8 | Large cartons, poly mailers |
| 6 × 6 | 152.4 × 152.4 | Boxes, large-format stickers |

Circles follow the same logic: 1 in (25 mm), 1.5 in (38 mm), 2 in (51 mm), 2.5 in (63 mm), 3 in (76 mm), 4 in (102 mm).

## Why standard sizes are cheaper

Three reasons, and only the first is about material:

1. **The die exists.** A custom shape needs a steel rule die above a few hundred units, or a plotter path at low volume. A standard size needs neither.
2. **The cut path is known.** No artwork setup to draw and check a dieline, which is usually a line item on a small order.
3. **Sheets and rolls are laid out for it.** Imposition is more efficient when the size matches a standard sheet division.

On a 50-unit order, a custom size can add 20–40% to the unit price through setup alone. On 5,000 units, the difference is noise.

## Metric buyers and inch sizes

If you work in millimetres, the inch list converts to awkward numbers. Two practical approaches:

- **Order the metric round number** — 50 × 75 mm instead of 50.8 × 76.2 mm. The 0.8 mm difference is invisible on a 50 mm label and avoids a conversion error in the artwork.
- **Keep the inch size but set up the file in mm** — 2 × 3 in becomes a 50.8 × 76.2 mm document with the artwork built to those numbers, not to 50 × 76.

The error to avoid is building the artwork at a rounded size and cutting at the exact one: the design shifts by a fraction of a millimetre on every edge, which matters most where a border or a rule sits near the trim.

## When to go custom

A custom size is worth the setup when:

- The container is not close to any standard size and a standard label would look either cramped or lost.
- The label has to carry mandatory text that does not fit in the nearest standard dimension.
- The shape is part of the brand — a contour cut, a tag shape, a die-cut window.
- The quantity is high enough that the die cost amortises: above roughly 500 units for a simple shape.

The reverse is also true: at 50 units, a standard size plus better artwork usually beats a custom die on both cost and speed.

## What this means for buyers

**Ask for the nearest standard size before asking for a custom one.** The saving is real, and most containers are within a few millimetres of a common size.

**Convert deliberately.** 25.4 mm per inch, and decide once whether the artwork is built to the exact or the rounded dimension.

**Do not assume "standard" means regulated.** It means commonly stocked. Ask which die exists rather than which size is "standard".

**Custom is a volume decision.** Below a few hundred units, the die is a large share of the order.
