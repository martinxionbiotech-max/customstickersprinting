A quoted lead time is a sum of steps, and most of them are not printing. Knowing the sequence tells you which delays are yours to control.

## The sequence

| Stage | Typical duration | Who controls it |
|---|---|---|
| Artwork check | 1 day | Supplier, needs your file |
| Proof and approval | 1–3 days | You |
| Print | 1–3 days | Supplier |
| Finishing (laminate, die, foil) | 1–3 days | Supplier |
| Packing | 1 day | Supplier |
| Shipping | 1–7 days | Carrier |

Our standard window is 7–12 working days from proof approval — not from order. That distinction is where most schedules slip.

## The two delays that are yours

**Slow approval.** A proof sitting in an inbox for four days adds four days to delivery. Nothing the printer does can recover it.

**Late artwork changes.** A revision after approval resets the proof and often the setup.

If a launch date matters, approve within 24 hours and freeze the file.

## Compressing a schedule honestly

- **Send print-ready artwork** — bleed, CMYK, outlined fonts, cut path. Each missing element costs a round trip.
- **Approve one proof, not three** — decide colour and content together.
- **Choose a simpler finish** — foil and spot UV add passes; a laminated label does not.
- **Order samples early** — a sample run is a separate short cycle; do it before the launch crunch, not during it.

Express production exists but it buys press time, not approval time.

## Shipping

Production lead time excludes transit. Domestic courier is typically 1–3 days; international express 3–7. If a delivery date is fixed, work backwards from it: shipping, then packing, then finishing, then print, then proof, then artwork.

## A realistic schedule for a first order

Work backwards from the date the labels must be in hand, not from the date you want to order. A first order has more steps than a reorder because the artwork, proof, material and die are all new.

| When | What happens | What you control |
|---|---|---|
| Week 0 | Specification and artwork sent | File quality, size, material decision |
| Day 1–2 | Artwork check and questions | Speed of answers |
| Day 2–4 | Proof issued and approved | Approval speed; this is the biggest variable |
| Day 4–7 | Print and finishing | Finish choice; foil and spot UV add passes |
| Day 7–8 | Packing | Packing format |
| Day 8–14 | Shipping | Destination, carrier, customs |

A 7–12 working day production window is realistic only if the proof is approved promptly. If the proof sits for four days, the delivery date moves by four days; express production cannot recover that time because it buys press time, not approval time.

For a launch, build in a buffer for the first order and keep the approved proof. The second order is faster because the artwork, proof and die already exist, and a reorder can often skip the proofing cycle if nothing has changed.

If a date is fixed and the first order is late, the honest options are to pay for a faster shipping method, split the delivery, or move the launch. Do not assume the printer can compress artwork approval and printing into the same day; that is how mistakes get approved.

## What this means for buyers

**The clock starts at approval, not at order.** Plan the approval into the schedule.

**Artwork quality is a lead-time variable.** A print-ready file removes a day and a round trip.

**Do not compress by skipping the sample.** It is faster to sample once than to reprint once.

**Build a buffer for the first order.** Second orders are faster because the proof and die already exist.

## The approval delay that express production cannot recover

A delivery date usually slips before the press starts. The buyer counts from the purchase order, while the supplier counts from an approved proof; the days spent answering artwork questions, waiting for a sample or changing a barcode sit between those two dates. Express production can buy press time, but it cannot approve the file for you.

The advice changes with the type of order. A reorder with the same approved artwork, material and die can move faster because those checks already exist. A first order with a new dieline, white ink or special finish needs time for a proof and a physical check. A launch date that leaves no room for one approval cycle is not protected by a faster courier; it is relying on nobody finding a problem.

Write the schedule backwards from the in-hand date: shipping, packing, finishing, print and proof, then assign a named approver and a response deadline. Ask what starts the lead-time clock and which changes reset it. A buffer is not padding; it is the time needed to answer a question without moving the delivery date. | Schedule risk | Where it enters | Buffer or proof to set |
|---|---|---|
| Unanswered artwork query | Pre-press check | Named contact and reply date |
| Proof waits for approval | Customer review | Approval deadline before order |
| Physical sample requested late | Separate sample cycle | Sample date in plan |
| New finish or die | Production setup | Added finishing time |
| Courier delay | Transit | Ship-by date with margin |
| Post-approval edit | File and proof reset | Frozen artwork handoff |
