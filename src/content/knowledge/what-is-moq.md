MOQ is one of the most misunderstood numbers in printing. It is not a policy imposed to annoy small buyers; it is the quantity below which the fixed costs of a job dominate the variable ones.

Understanding the arithmetic explains both why minimums exist and why ours is 50.

## Fixed and variable cost

Every print job has costs that do not change with quantity:

- Artwork preparation and proofing
- Plates (flexo) or file setup (digital)
- Press make-ready and colour adjustment
- Die cutting setup
- Packing setup

And costs that scale with quantity: material, ink, cutting time, packing.

At 50 units, the fixed costs are spread over 50 items. At 50,000, they are spread over 50,000. The material cost per label barely changes; the setup cost per label collapses.

## Why the industry quotes in thousands

Flexographic printing needs a plate per colour and a make-ready per job. That setup is roughly the same whether the run is 500 or 500,000, so printers push quantity up to make the unit price presentable.

Below their threshold, the honest unit price looks absurd, so they decline rather than quote.

Digital printing removes the plates. Setup drops to file preparation and a short make-ready, which is what makes a 50-unit minimum economically possible — not generosity, arithmetic.

## What 50 units covers

- A first batch for a launch or a market test
- Several designs combined in one order, if material and size match
- Samples for photography, a trade show or a buyer meeting
- A seasonal or limited variant without leftover stock

It does not cover a national retail rollout. For that, the per-unit price at 2,000+ is materially better and the job usually moves to flexo.

## The real comparison

A 50-unit order costs more per label than a 5,000-unit order. That is not a markup; it is the same setup divided by a different number.

The honest comparison is between two strategies:

| Strategy | Unit cost | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| 50 units | High | Low — validate before committing |
| 5,000 units | Low | High — wrong design, dead stock |

For a first product, the low-risk option is usually worth the higher unit cost. For a proven design, the reverse.

## What this means for buyers

**Ask what the minimum includes.** A minimum that excludes setup, die or proofing is not a minimum, it is a deposit.

**Combine designs to reach the minimum.** Three designs that share a material and size can be one order.

**Do not treat MOQ as a quality signal.** A low minimum usually means digital printing, which is a method, not a compromise.

**Plan the second order before the first.** The reorder skips proofing and moves faster; knowing that changes how you budget the launch.
