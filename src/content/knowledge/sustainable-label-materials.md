Sustainability language is easy to print and hard to support. A label buyer may be asked for “eco-friendly stock”, “plastic-free packaging” or a “compostable label” before anyone has defined which part of the construction is being discussed. The result can be a wording change that sounds responsible but cannot be traced to a material, a certificate or an end-of-life route.

This is production guidance, not legal advice. Environmental claims are regulated differently by market and can be enforced by authorities or challenged by competitors or customers. The brand should obtain its own review; confirm the applicable rules with the importer or a qualified adviser. See the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/). Part of the [specialty labels guide](/knowledge/specialty-labels/).

## Define the claim before the material

Write down the exact sentence the brand wants to use. “Made with FSC-certified paper”, “contains 30% post-consumer recycled fibre”, “linerless format” and “industrially compostable” are not interchangeable. Each points to a different component, a different evidence trail and a different end-of-life expectation.

Then define the scope. Does the claim describe the face stock, the liner, the adhesive, the printed label as a whole or the packaged product? A supplier may be able to document one part while the final pack cannot support a broader statement. The label should not carry a claim that the underlying packaging contradicts.

Finally, decide who owns the evidence. The brand usually owns the public claim; the printer or material supplier provides the chain-of-custody record, certificate or technical document. Keep those records with the approved artwork and review them before the claim is repeated in a new market or on a new pack.

## FSC-certified paper and chain of custody

The Forest Stewardship Council sets standards for responsible forest management and a chain-of-custody system that tracks certified material through the supply chain. For a label, the relevant question is whether the paper component can be supplied with the correct chain-of-custody status and whether the printer is authorised to make the related claim on the print.

We do not hold FSC chain-of-custody certification for our own operation. If a job needs an FSC claim, it has to be produced under a certified chain of custody and supported by the producer's certificate and invoice wording; an FSC logo added to artwork is not evidence of certification. See the [declarations page](/declarations/) for the documents we can and cannot provide.

FSC certification is not a statement that the whole label is compostable, recyclable or free of chemicals. It is also not a licence to use the FSC logo without following the applicable rules. Ask which certificate covers the material, what the certificate scope includes and which document will accompany the order.

Paper choice affects more than certification. A coated or uncoated sheet changes the print result; a thin sheet can cockle in humid service; a textured stock may need a different laminate or no laminate at all. Choose the paper for the label’s job, then confirm the documentation behind the environmental claim.

## Recycled content: post-consumer and pre-consumer

Recycled content can describe material recovered after consumer use or waste recovered before it reaches the consumer. The distinction matters to the credibility of the claim and to the supply chain. Ask for the definition used by the supplier, the percentage claimed and whether it applies to the face stock, the liner or the complete construction.

Recycled fibre can vary in shade, strength and print response. That is not necessarily a defect, but it should be visible in the approved sample. If the design depends on a bright white background, a recycled sheet may need more ink or a different print treatment. Test the actual stock rather than approving it from a colour chip.

Recycled content does not automatically mean the label can go into a paper recycling stream. The adhesive, liner, coating and container still matter. Check the recovery route for the complete item, not just the material name.

## Linerless labels: less carrier material

A conventional roll label has a release liner that protects the adhesive and is removed during application. A linerless construction removes that carrier, which can reduce the material that has to be handled and discarded. It can also allow more labels per roll and change packing and transport requirements.

Linerless labels are not a drop-in replacement for every line. The adhesive must release from the roll and cut cleanly on the applicator; the equipment may need a different setup; and the available face stocks and finishes may be narrower. The buyer should test the roll on the actual applicator and inspect the first and last labels, not only a hand-applied sample.

If the main environmental concern is liner waste, linerless is a direct discussion. If the concern is whether the label can be recycled with the container, linerless does not answer that question by itself.

## Thinner films and material reduction

A thinner film can reduce the amount of material in the label, but it can also change stiffness, application behaviour, opacity and durability. A thinner face may conform better to a curved container or may wrinkle more easily under a squeegee. It may run differently on a high-speed applicator. The specification should include the face, adhesive, liner and application method, not only a micron figure.

Source reduction is generally easier to defend than a vague “green” claim because it is measurable at the material level. Even then, the buyer should ask what happens to the rest of the construction and whether the thinner material changes the service life. A label that fails early is not a lower-impact outcome.

| Option | Potential benefit | Trade-off to verify |
|---|---|---|
| FSC-certified paper | Documented forest and chain-of-custody route for the paper | Does not cover adhesive, liner, ink or end-of-life |
| Recycled fibre | Reuses recovered material | Shade, strength and print response can vary |
| Linerless roll | Avoids a separate release liner | Needs compatible equipment and adhesive release |
| Thinner film | Less material by area | Stiffness, opacity and runnability can change |
| Water-based adhesive | Different carrier chemistry | Not automatically compostable or recyclable |

## Water-based adhesive is not a finish line

Water-based adhesives use water as part of the carrier system. That fact alone does not establish a composting, recycling or biodegradability outcome. The adhesive must still bond to the container, survive the service condition and be compatible with any recovery route.

Ask why the water-based option is being proposed. If the concern is solvent exposure in the converting process, the supplier can explain the relevant chemistry and controls. If the concern is the label’s end of life, the question is the complete construction and the waste system, not the word “water-based”.

## What “compostable” does and does not mean

Compostable means that a material or product can break down under defined composting conditions within a defined time, leaving a specified result. It is not a synonym for biodegradable, recyclable or “natural”. The claim should identify the environment: home composting and industrial composting are different routes with different conditions.

The whole label matters. A compostable face with a conventional adhesive, liner or laminate may not meet the intended specification. The printed ink and the container also form part of the real system. Ask for the standard, the certification scope and the specific component covered, then check whether the local waste system accepts that route.

| Claim | What it can mean | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| FSC-certified | The paper is covered by a forest certification and chain-of-custody system | Certificate scope, supplier status and claim wording |
| Recycled content | Some or all material is recovered rather than virgin | Percentage, type and component covered |
| Linerless | No separate release liner on the roll | Applicator compatibility and adhesive release |
| Compostable | Designed to break down in a defined composting environment | Standard, certification, components and local acceptance |
| Recyclable | Compatible with a specified recovery route | Collection system, full construction and container |

## Greenwashing boundaries

An environmental claim should be specific, substantiated and limited to what the buyer can actually prove. A general phrase such as “eco-friendly” gives the reader no basis for comparison. A claim that implies the whole package is recyclable when only the face stock has a relevant attribute is misleading even if the supplier used that phrase informally.

The US FTC Green Guides provide guidance on environmental marketing claims, including qualifications and the need for evidence. Other markets have their own rules. Treat any claim that will appear on a printed label as a marketing and compliance decision, not a printer preference.

## Questions to ask a label supplier

Ask which component the option covers, what standard or programme supports it, who holds the evidence and when it expires. Ask whether the material changes print colour, adhesion, application speed, shelf life or finish. Ask for a sample on the actual container and a document that the buyer can file.

The strongest brief is usually modest: a precise material change, a defined evidence pack and a statement of the trade-off. That is easier to defend than a label covered in symbols that nobody can explain.
