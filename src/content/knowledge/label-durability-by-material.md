"Is this label durable?" is unanswerable without a condition and a duration. The same material can last five years on a warehouse shelf and three weeks in a shower.

## Expected life by material and condition

| Material | Indoor dry | Chilled / humid | Frozen | Outdoor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coated paper | Years | Weeks | Days | Days |
| Water-resistant paper | Years | 1–3 months | Not suitable | Not suitable |
| PP | Years | Years | 1–2 years | 6–12 months |
| PET | Years | Years | 1–2 years | 1–2 years |
| Vinyl (sticker) | Years | Years | 1–2 years | 2–5 years, laminated |

These are ranges, not guarantees: adhesive, lamination and surface preparation move the outcome more than the film alone.

## What shortens label life

- **Condensation** — water at the edge is where lift starts.
- **Oil and solvents** — soften adhesive and attack print.
- **UV** — fades ink and embrittles film; lamination is the buffer.
- **Abrasion** — handling, packing, transport.
- **Temperature cycling** — freeze–thaw stresses adhesive and laminate.
- **Curved, small diameters** — constant peel force at the edges.

## Lamination as a multiplier

On PP or vinyl, a laminate can multiply service life several times over by keeping water and abrasion away from the ink. On paper it helps but cannot rescue an unsuitable material.

If a label must last outdoors or in a bathroom, laminate is not a cosmetic upgrade — it is the durability specification.

## How to test

A material data sheet describes a film in a lab, not your product in your conditions. The practical test:

1. Apply a sample to the actual container.
2. Store it exactly as the product is stored.
3. Inspect at 24 hours, one week and one month.
4. Look at the edges, not the centre — failure starts there.

A month of real testing is worth more than any datasheet, and it costs one sample run.

## A one-month test protocol

A datasheet describes a film in a laboratory. Your product lives in a fridge, a bathroom or a shop window. The cheapest way to close that gap is a one-month test on the actual container.

Use three samples: one stored exactly like the product, one in the harshest condition the product may see, and one control kept indoors. Label them with the date and the material. Inspect at 24 hours, one week and four weeks. At each check, look at the edges first, then the print, then the adhesive.

Record four things: edge lift, colour change, surface contamination and adhesive transfer. A label can look perfect in the centre while the edge has already lifted; that edge is where water and oil get under the film. Photograph the samples under the same light each time so the comparison is honest.

If the label fails, change one variable at a time: adhesive first, then laminate, then face stock. Changing all three at once gives you a result but not a reason. If it passes, keep the control sample as the reference for the next run.

## What this means for buyers

**State the condition and the duration.** "Must survive six months in a fridge" is a specification; "durable" is not.

**Budget lamination where life matters.** It is a small share of unit cost and the largest single factor in service life on film.

**Paper is a short-life material outdoors, whatever the coating.** If the product travels, specify film.

**Test the edges.** A label that looks perfect in the centre can be lifting at the edge, which is where it will fail.

## How to tell a material failure from a service-condition mismatch

A label that fails in service is not automatically the wrong film. The same paper can last years in a dry box and days in a shower; the same PP can survive a fridge and lift on a greasy bottle. Before changing material, separate what failed: the print, the face stock, the laminate, the adhesive or the application. Each points to a different correction, and changing all of them at once hides the cause.

The expected life changes with the condition and the amount of handling. UV exposure, abrasion, oil, condensation and freeze-thaw cycling attack the construction in different ways. A laminate may protect ink from scuffing while doing little for a poor adhesive bond; a stronger film may resist tearing while still lifting at the edge. The useful brief names the condition and the duration, then chooses the lowest-cost construction that meets both.

Run a control beside the test label, photograph both under the same light, and record edge lift, colour change, surface contamination and adhesive transfer at fixed intervals. Change one variable at a time and keep the passing sample as a reference. | Failure observation | Likely layer | Next check |
|---|---|---|
| Print fades before the edge lifts | Ink or laminate | UV and abrasion exposure |
| Edge lifts while centre is intact | Adhesive or application | Surface and pressure |
| Face stock cockles | Paper or humidity | Moisture and storage |
| Label cracks when cold | Adhesive flexibility | Freezer test |
| Residue remains after removal | Adhesive softness | Removal surface and time |
