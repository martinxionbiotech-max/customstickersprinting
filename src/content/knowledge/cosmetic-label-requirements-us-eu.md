Cosmetics labels look simpler than food labels until the same bottle is sold in more than one market. A front panel may carry the brand, product name, shade and volume; the back panel then has to hold an ingredient list, a business address, warnings, a batch code and sometimes a date or period-after-opening symbol. On a 30 ml dropper or a slim lipstick carton, that is a lot of information for a small area.

This is general production information, not legal advice. The brand or responsible person must confirm the final text and market route; read the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/). Part of the [label compliance guide](/knowledge/label-compliance/).

## The two questions that set the label

The first question is what the product is in each market. A cosmetic claim can move a product toward the drug boundary, which changes the required warnings and possibly the regulatory route. The second question is who is answerable for the product there. In the US, the label identifies the manufacturer, packer or distributor. In the EU, the responsible person must be established in the EU or European Economic Area and identified on the label.

Those two answers determine more of the artwork than the brand palette does. They decide whether a warning block is needed, which address is printed, whether the ingredient list changes and which market-specific symbols or dates appear. Settle them before the dieline is chosen.

## US: identity, net quantity and the information panel

The FDA's cosmetic labelling rules are in 21 CFR Parts 701 and 740. The principal display panel normally carries the identity of the cosmetic and the net quantity. The information panel carries the ingredient declaration and the name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer or distributor, plus any warning required for the product.

Net quantity must be stated in the required units and placed as the regulation requires. The business address is not just a brand name or a website; the label needs the entity information specified in Part 701. If a distributor is named rather than the manufacturer, the qualifying wording must follow the current rule. Do not let a template from another product decide that wording.

## US: ingredients and warning statements

The ingredient declaration lists ingredients in descending order of predominance, with the regulation's exceptions for fragrances, flavours and certain trade-secret situations. Colourants have their own order rules. The names must follow the nomenclature the FDA permits or recognises; INCI names are often used, but the brand should confirm the declaration against the current rule rather than relying on a supplier's old template.

Warnings are product-specific. 21 CFR Part 740 contains warning requirements for certain cosmetics, and other product characteristics can trigger additional statements. Not every cosmetic needs the same warning block, and a warning copied from another product can be wrong. The brand should obtain the applicable warning text and confirm it with the FDA source or a qualified adviser.

## EU: the responsible person and the label particulars

Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 requires the label to identify the responsible person and provide the product's required particulars. These commonly include the name and address of the responsible person, nominal content, the ingredient list, the batch identification, the date of minimum durability or period after opening where applicable, warnings, and the function of the product where it is not clear from its presentation.

The responsible person is a substantive role, not a mailbox added to artwork. The named entity must be able to hold the product information file and respond to the market authority. Before the label is laid out, the brand should know the exact legal name and address that will appear and confirm that the entity accepts the role.

## EU: INCI, dates and the language of the market

The EU ingredient declaration uses the common ingredient nomenclature and follows the regulation's order and grouping rules. Allergenic fragrance substances may need special emphasis depending on the concentration and the current rule. The brand's safety assessor and regulatory adviser should approve the final list rather than a designer or printer reconstructing it from a marketing brief.

The date or period-after-opening presentation depends on the product's minimum durability and packaging. Warnings and other mandatory particulars must be in the language of the member state where the product is made available, or in a language easily understood there. A multi-language back panel can grow quickly, especially when the responsible-person address and warnings are repeated.

## Field comparison: US and EU

| Field | United States | European Union |
|---|---|---|
| Product identity | Required on the principal display panel | Product name and function where needed |
| Net or nominal content | Required net quantity | Required nominal content |
| Ingredients | Ingredient declaration with prescribed order and naming | Ingredient list using the common nomenclature and grouping rules |
| Responsible entity | Manufacturer, packer or distributor name and place of business | Responsible person established in the EU/EEA, with name and address |
| Batch or lot | Product-specific traceability requirement; confirm the applicable rule | Batch identification required |
| Durability or opening date | Where required for the product | Minimum durability date or period after opening where applicable |
| Warnings | Product-specific requirements in Part 740 and other rules | Required warnings and any product-specific statements |
| Language | English generally, subject to limited exceptions | Language of the market or a language easily understood there |
| Small-pack alternative | Limited provisions; confirm before relying on them | Limited practical alternatives; confirm applicability |

## Small packages and professional use

Small packages are a layout problem before they are a regulatory one. A 5 ml vial, a sample sachet or a pencil carton may not have enough printable surface for the same panel as a 200 ml bottle. The first response should be a larger carton, a folded leaflet or a multi-panel label. The second is to check whether the market provides a limited alternative for the package, and whether the brand qualifies.

Professional-use products add another layer. Salon, spa or clinic use can change the required warnings, the language and the way the product is labelled for transport and storage. It does not remove the need for the consumer-facing particulars when the product is also sold to the public. Confirm the intended use and channel in writing before deciding which version of the artwork is the master.

For a product sold both professionally and at retail, keep the professional and consumer versions as separate approved files. A professional-use statement or a larger warning block can push the back panel beyond its available area, and an overlabel added later may cover a required ingredient or batch field. It is cheaper to design the longest version first and simplify only where the market permits.

## What the printer can decide, and what it cannot

| Question | Printer can | Brand or responsible person must |
|---|---|---|
| Does the text fit? | Measure the safe area and flag overflow at the agreed type size | Decide which fields are mandatory and which copy can move |
| Is the ingredient block legible? | Reproduce the supplied list and flag small type or low contrast | Approve the ingredient names, order and grouping |
| Is the responsible-party address correct? | Set the approved address exactly as supplied | Confirm the legal entity and the market where it applies |
| Is the date or batch visible? | Place the variable data in the allocated area | Choose the date type and batch format required for the product |
| Is the warning complete? | Check that the supplied warning fits and remains readable | Obtain and approve the warning text for the product and market |
| Does the barcode scan? | Preserve quiet zones and check a printed proof | Assign the correct GTIN and confirm the retail requirement |

## Artwork checks for a cosmetics back panel

Put the ingredient list, responsible-party block, warnings and variable data on one layer or one clearly marked text group. Keep the approved text in a separate file from the design, with the language and version named. Check the back panel at final size, not zoomed in on a desktop screen. If the label will be clear or metallic, check how the finish changes contrast for small text.

The [label size calculator](/tools/label-size-calculator/) can test the available wrap area before the layout is locked. For another regulated category with a similar field-first method, see the [food label guide](/knowledge/food-label-requirements-us-eu/).

## Questions to settle before print

1. Which entity is the manufacturer, packer, distributor or responsible person for each market?
2. Which claims are approved, and do any change the product's regulatory route?
3. Which ingredient declaration and naming system has been approved?
4. Which date, period-after-opening and batch format applies to this package?
5. Which language versions are approved, and which one is the longest?

The answers should exist before a final proof. If the brand cannot answer them, the honest next step is to hold the print rather than let a supplier guess. The printer can keep the layout flexible; it cannot make an unapproved claim or an incomplete ingredient list safe by printing it neatly.
