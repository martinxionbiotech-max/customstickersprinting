A wine or spirits label is both a brand asset and a market-entry document. On a 750 ml bottle, the front label must work on a shelf and in a photograph, while the back label carries the destination market's information without becoming a folded legal brief. Resolve that tension before the bottle and label size are fixed.

This is general production information, not legal advice. The producer, importer and authorised agent must confirm the current label approval and market rules; read the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/). Part of the [label compliance guide](/knowledge/label-compliance/).

## Start with the market and the route to market

The first decision is where the bottle will be sold and who is responsible there. A tasting-room bottle, a US retail bottle covered by a TTB certificate of label approval, and an EU retail bottle with an importer's details are three different labelling jobs. They may share a front design, not a back panel.

Ask whether the product needs a COLA, whether the destination uses a protected designation or another regulated term, and which entity must appear as bottler, producer or importer. That determines the field list before dimensions are chosen.

Spirits follow different TTB class and type requirements and different EU product rules. Check the specific beverage with TTB and the importer before reusing a wine template.

## US TTB COLA basics

TTB's certificate of label approval process reviews certain alcohol beverage labels before they are used in interstate commerce. The application brings together the brand, class and type, alcohol content, net contents, bottler or importer information, origin where relevant, sulfite declaration where required and government warning.

The approved label and the printed label need to match. A change to the brand, class or type, alcohol content or origin can require a new review. Confirm the route and whether a change needs a new COLA with TTB or a qualified adviser. The [TTB wine labelling pages](https://www.ttb.gov/labeling/wine-labeling) and 27 CFR Part 4 are starting references.

## The US fields a producer should map

The following list is a practical field map, not a substitute for the current TTB requirements:

- Brand name.
- Class and type designation.
- Alcohol content, in the form required for the product.
- Net contents.
- Name and address of the bottler, producer or importer, with the required qualifying wording.
- Country of origin where applicable.
- Sulfite declaration where the threshold is met.
- The government warning.

Some wines have additional appellation, varietal or vintage rules. The producer should confirm which statements are mandatory, optional or prohibited for the specific wine and label. A brand name that looks like a varietal or appellation term can create a separate review problem.

## The US government warning

The alcohol health warning is a controlled statement. The text should be taken from 27 CFR Part 16 at the time of artwork approval, not copied from an older label. The required statement is:

> GOVERNMENT WARNING: (1) According to the Surgeon General, women should not drink alcoholic beverages during pregnancy because of the risk of birth defects. (2) Consumption of alcoholic beverages impairs your ability to drive a car or operate machinery, and may cause health problems.

The warning has type-size, contrast and placement requirements. On a dark or textured label, the statement can be technically present but visually lost. Check the proof at final size and on the intended stock. A printer can preserve the supplied block and flag a contrast problem; the producer and importer must confirm the wording and the approval route.

## EU wine labelling basics

EU wine labelling sits within the common agricultural market rules, with wine-specific provisions for regulated terms, geographical indications, the actual alcoholic strength, lot identification, bottler or producer details, allergens and the language of the market. Sulphite information is a common allergen consideration for wine.

The rules also interact with the product category and any protected designation. A term that is ordinary in one market may be regulated in another. The importer should confirm the current mandatory particulars, the permitted optional terms and the presentation requirements for the destination member state. The EU [Regulation 1308/2013](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2013/1308/oj/eng) is the broad reference; the current delegated and implementing rules should be checked with it.

## US and EU fields compared

| Field | United States, TTB route | European Union, wine route |
|---|---|---|
| Product identity | Brand, class and type as approved | Product category and regulated terms where used |
| Alcohol content | Required in the TTB form for the product | Actual alcoholic strength required |
| Net contents | Required | Required under the applicable packaging rules |
| Responsible party | Bottler, producer or importer with qualifying wording | Bottler, producer or importer details required for the market |
| Origin | Country of origin and appellation rules where applicable | Origin or geographical indication where applicable |
| Sulfites | Declaration where the threshold is met | Allergen information, including sulphites where applicable |
| Lot or batch | Product traceability requirement; confirm the TTB and market rule | Lot identification required |
| Health warning | US government warning under 27 CFR Part 16 | EU and national requirements; confirm with the importer |
| Language | English and any TTB-approved wording | Language of the market or a language easily understood there |

## Label geometry on a 750 ml bottle

A 750 ml bottle is not a uniform cylinder. The shoulder, neck, heel and punt reduce the straight printable band, and the circumference changes through the shoulder. A front label that starts too high can cross into the curve and lift at the top edge. A wrap that assumes a straight cylinder can bunch or leave a visible seam.

Measure the bottle mould: straight-wall height, centre-line circumference and distance from seams or the punt. Decide whether the label stays on the flat front or wraps. Size from the printable band, not a stock size. Use the [label size calculator](/tools/label-size-calculator/), and ask for a proof on the actual stock.

Check the back label separately. A tall label may not sit flat on a pronounced shoulder or near a deep punt. The height needed for importer details and a market warning can decide between a single label, a two-label set and a neck tag.

## Paper versus synthetic on condensation

Condensation is the common failure mode for a chilled bottle. A paper label can cockle, lift at the edge or lose print if moisture reaches the adhesive or the face. A synthetic face stock may resist water better, but it still needs an adhesive that works on the bottle surface and under cold, wet conditions.

| Service condition | Material direction to discuss | What to test |
|---|---|---|
| Dry retail shelf and controlled cellar | Paper with a suitable print protection | Normal handling, abrasion and label appearance |
| Ice bucket or repeated condensation | Synthetic or moisture-resistant construction with a compatible adhesive | Immersion, condensation, edge lift and print resistance |
| Premium textured or uncoated look | Paper, with a finish that preserves the intended feel while protecting the print | Whether the finish changes colour, texture or scuff resistance |
| Export cartons and transit | Construction that resists abrasion and label-to-label contact | Packing, rubbing and the actual transit conditions |
| Tasting-room hand washing | Finish and adhesive that suit repeated handling | Wet hands, wiping and the service method |

No material name alone guarantees performance. Face stock, adhesive, laminate and bottle surface work as a system. Ask for a sample on the actual bottle and test the service condition that matters, not a generic water splash.

## Tasting-room identity versus export SKU

A tasting-room label can tell the producer's story. An export SKU must also carry the importer, destination language, applicable warning and market-specific terms while still looking like the same wine. Reusing the tasting-room label with an added sticker can serve a small trial, but it creates a second approved version and risks the wrong label reaching the wrong case.

Build the export back panel first; it is usually the more constrained surface. Then simplify for the tasting room only where the market allows. Keep the front design and back content as named versions, and record which bottle and market each version belongs to. The [food label article](/knowledge/food-label-requirements-us-eu/) shows a similar field-matrix method.

## What the printer can decide, and what it cannot

| Question | Printer can | Producer, importer or agent must |
|---|---|---|
| Will the warning fit? | Set the supplied warning at the required size and flag overflow | Confirm the current wording and the required presentation |
| Is the label on the right bottle? | Provide a proof on the actual stock and bottle shape | Confirm the bottle, market and approved label version |
| Is the material suitable for condensation? | Recommend constructions and prepare test samples | Approve the construction after testing the service condition |
| Is the approved COLA matched? | Reproduce the approved artwork and flag a version mismatch | Confirm that the COLA and the printed label agree |
| Is the importer block correct? | Set the supplied entity details exactly | Confirm the importer, address and qualifying wording |
| Is the barcode scannable? | Preserve quiet zones and verify the printed proof | Assign the correct GTIN and confirm the retail requirement |

## Questions to settle before print

1. Which market and route require a COLA, importer block or other approval?
2. Which product terms, geographical indications and warnings are approved?
3. Which bottle mould and which straight-wall band will the label use?
4. Will the bottle be chilled, iced or handled wet, and which construction survives that service?
5. Which versions belong to the tasting room, the domestic market and each export market?

The answers should be attached to the proof and to the approved label file. A wine label that works on the bottle but not in the market is still a production failure, however good the paper feels in the hand.
