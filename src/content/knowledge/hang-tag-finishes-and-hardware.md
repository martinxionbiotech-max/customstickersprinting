A hang tag can be printed beautifully and still fail as a product because the finish feels wrong or the hardware tears out. The finish and the attachment are not decoration; they are the parts the buyer touches.

## Surface finishes

**Matte laminate** is the default for retail tags. It hides fingerprints, takes a pen and feels soft without being slippery. It slightly reduces colour contrast, so a very dark or very bright design may look a little flatter than on gloss. For most apparel and candle tags, matte is the safer choice.

**Gloss laminate** makes colour pop and can look more premium on a bright design. It shows handling marks and fingerprints more than matte, and it can look cheap if the card underneath is thin. Use it when the design depends on saturation, not as a default.

**Uncoated or varnished** card has a tactile, natural feel. It suits kraft, recycled and craft brands, and it takes a pen or a stamp. The trade-off is lower resistance to handling and moisture; a tag that will be handled on a retail floor may look tired quickly.

**Soft-touch laminate** feels velvety and photographs well, but it shows fingerprints and can scuff. It is a premium signal for a beauty or fragrance brand; it is a poor choice for a tag that will be handled with oily hands.

## Foil, emboss and spot UV

Foil adds a metallic element — gold, silver, copper or a coloured foil. It needs a die and a make-ready, and the artwork needs a solid area rather than a hairline. Fine reverse-out type in foil can fill in; a bold mark or a border works better. On uncoated or textured card, foil can look pitted; a coated or laminated surface gives a cleaner result.

Emboss raises the card in a shaped die. It needs enough card weight to hold the shape: 350 gsm is a practical minimum, and 400 gsm is better for a deep emboss. A embossed logo with a foil overprint is a common premium combination, but it needs two passes and a die, so the quantity has to support it.

Spot UV adds a gloss highlight on a matte surface. It needs a screen and a pass, and it is usually economical at higher quantities. On a small tag, the highlight can be lost; on a large tag, it can guide the eye to the logo. It is a design decision, not a durability upgrade.

## Eyelets and holes

An eyelet is a metal ring set into the hole. It protects the card from the string, spreads the load and makes the tag feel finished. Brass, silver, black and coloured eyelets are available; the colour should match the hardware on the product or the string.

Without an eyelet, a 4 mm hole in 350 gsm card can ovalise after handling. The string cuts into the card, the hole grows and the tag hangs crooked. If the tag is a keeper or will be handled on a retail floor, an eyelet is worth the small cost.

The hole position matters as much as the eyelet. Keep 8–10 mm from the top edge and 3 mm of clear space around the hole. A larger eyelet needs more clearance; a small tag with a large eyelet can look out of proportion.

## String, cord and elastic

**Cotton string** is the classic choice: soft, cheap and available in many colours. It knots securely but can fray at the cut; a waxed end or a knot cover helps.

**Waxed cord** slides through the hole and knots tightly. It looks more premium than plain cotton and resists moisture, but the wax can mark a light-coloured card if the tag is packed tightly.

**Elastic** stretches over a product and holds the tag close. It is useful for socks, gloves and items that cannot hang from a rail, but it can pull the tag against the product and crease it.

**Safety pins** avoid the hole entirely and suit garments that cannot be pierced or tagged in the usual way. They are slower to attach and can be a safety consideration for children’s products.

## Quantity economics

Every special finish adds a setup: a foil die, an emboss die, a spot UV screen, a custom eyelet colour. At 50 units, those setups can be a large share of the order. At 1,000 units, they are spread across more pieces and the unit price falls.

The practical sequence for a first order is:

1. Choose the card and the cut.
2. Add a matte laminate if the tag will be handled.
3. Add an eyelet if the tag will hang or be kept.
4. Add foil or emboss only when the design and quantity justify the die.
5. Add spot UV last; it is a highlight, not a structural improvement.

## What this means for buyers

**Finish is not durability.** A laminate protects the print, but it does not stop a thin card from tearing at the hole. Match the card and the eyelet to the handling.

**Foil and emboss need a die.** Ask for the die cost separately and compare it with the quantity. A 50-unit order with foil can cost more than a 500-unit order without it.

**Choose the attachment with the product.** A string, elastic or pin changes how the tag hangs and how the product is handled. Decide it before the hole is placed.

**Keep the hardware consistent.** Eyelet colour, string colour and card finish should read as one object. A silver eyelet with a gold foil logo looks like two decisions made separately.

## The premium finish that loses its buyer at the eyelet

A tag can carry an expensive finish and still feel unfinished when the hardware is picked up. Foil near the hole can crack as the eyelet is set, a stiff laminate can make the card curl, a bright silver eyelet can fight a warm foil, and a thin string can fray or pull through. The buyer handles the attachment before reading the brand, so an assembly failure outweighs the surface beauty.

Card weight, hardware size and carrying method alter the assembly. A soft-touch laminate changes the feel and shows oil, while an uncoated kraft tag accepts a stamp but marks easily. A large eyelet spreads the load but needs more clearance. Elastic can pull the tag against the product, and a long string changes the angle at the hole. The finish, card and attachment have to be tested as one object.

The assembled sample should use the production card, finish, eyelet and string. Pull, twist and hang it, then inspect the finish immediately around the hole as well as the printed face. Confirm the eyelet barrel diameter, colour reference and string construction in writing.
