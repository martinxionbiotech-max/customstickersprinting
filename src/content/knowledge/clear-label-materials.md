A clear label is a film, a print and a decision about opacity. Most of the cost and all of the visual result come from that third part.

## Why colour needs white

CMYK inks are translucent. Printed directly onto clear film and applied to a coloured product, they mix optically with whatever is behind them: a red logo on amber liquid reads brown, white text on dark glass disappears.

A white ink layer printed behind the design restores opacity. It is a separate pass, and it is the difference between a label that looks designed and one that looks like a sticker.

## Three white-ink strategies

| Strategy | Result | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Full flood | Entire label opaque, clear margin visible | Text-heavy labels, dark products |
| Spot white | Only logo and text opaque, rest transparent | The "floating" premium look |
| No white | Everything translucent | Subtle tints, deliberate effects |

Spot white needs a defined layer in the artwork. Without it, the printer guesses — and guessing produces either a flood (losing the effect) or an unreadable logo.

## Matching film to container

**Clear PP** is the flexible standard, good on curved bottles and squeeze containers.

**Clear PET** is stiffer and clearer, better for flat glass, spirits and premium cosmetics.

**Matte clear film** gives a frosted effect that hides bubbles and fingerprints — often the more forgiving choice on a hand-applied label.

## Where clear works, and where it does not

| Container | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Smooth clear glass | Best case |
| Frosted glass | Works if the frost is intended |
| Dark glass or plastic | Needs a white flood or it disappears |
| Textured or recycled board | Poor — film edges catch light |
| Kraft packaging | Looks like a mistake |

## Application quality

Clear film shows bubbles, dust and misalignment that white stock conceals. For runs above a few hundred on small containers, an applicator gives a visibly cleaner result; hand application needs a clean, dry surface and a squeegee from the centre out.

## Designing for clear film

Clear film is less forgiving than white stock because everything behind it shows: the container, the product, bubbles, dust and the adhesive itself. The design has to decide which parts are transparent and which parts are opaque.

White ink is the opacity layer. It can sit behind the whole design (a flood coat) or only behind the areas that need to be solid (spot white). A flood coat gives a consistent background and hides the container; spot white keeps the no-label look but requires a separate layer and registration. Without white ink, colours print directly on clear film and read translucent; a dark bottle can swallow a dark logo.

Bubbles are an application issue, not a printing issue. A clean, smooth surface and a conformable film help; a stiff film on a curved bottle traps air at the edge. If the label is applied by hand, a squeegee and a slow, centre-out technique make more difference than a different adhesive.

For a first sample, apply the clear label to the actual container and look at it against both a light and a dark background. A design that reads well on white paper can disappear on glass.

## What this means for buyers

**Decide the white areas in the artwork, not in the quote.** A clear label without a white-ink layer is a design decision that was never made.

**Clear is a premium, not a default.** It costs more than white film, and it only pays off on a container that suits it.

**Matte clear hides what gloss clear reveals.** On hand-applied labels, matte is usually the kinder choice.

**Ask for a sample on the actual container.** Transparent film on a white desk looks perfect; on amber glass it may not.

## Where clear film turns a printing choice into an application problem

A clear label can be approved on a white layout and fail on the container. The white ink layer may be missing, too narrow behind thin type, or flooded in a way that removes the transparent effect the buyer wanted. Once applied, bubbles, dust and edge lift are more visible than they would be under white film. The result is blamed on the print when the cause is the construction or the application.

The advice changes with the container and the viewing angle. Smooth clear glass gives the film its best chance; dark glass needs enough white ink to keep text legible; textured board and kraft make the edge stand out and rarely justify the premium. Gloss clear reveals imperfections, while matte clear softens them. Hand application needs a clean surface and a slow centre-out technique, especially on a curved bottle.

Ask for the white-ink strategy as a separate artwork layer, then request a sample on the actual container viewed against both light and dark backgrounds. Inspect the edges after application and again the next day, when trapped air and poor wet-out are easier to see. | Clear-label symptom | Printing cause | Application cause |
|---|---|---|
| Text disappears on dark glass | Insufficient white ink | Wrong viewing background |
| Design looks milky | Flood white too wide | Bubbles under the film |
| Logo edge looks soft | White registration | Stretching on a curve |
| Label lifts at one edge | Cut or material mismatch | Dust or incomplete pressure |
| Surface looks uneven | Ink density variation | Textured container |
