Retail is the channel where a label stops being marketing and becomes a compliance document. The requirements are not decorative: a missing net quantity or business address can stop a product being listed, and the label size needed to carry them is larger than most DTC designs allow.

Part of the [marketplace label requirements guide](/knowledge/marketplace-label-requirements/).

## The baseline: three items

In the United States, the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act requires consumer packages to carry:

| Item | What it means |
|---|---|
| Identity | What the product is, in plain terms |
| Net quantity | Weight, volume or count, in metric and US units |
| Business name and address | Manufacturer, packer or distributor — a street address, not just a brand name |

Imported goods also need a **country of origin** marking, which is a customs requirement rather than a labelling one, and is checked at import as well as at retail.

That is the floor. Food, cosmetics, supplements and children's products add their own layers, and those layers are where labels run out of space.

## Food in the EU: the longer list

Regulation 1169/2011 requires, among other items:

- The name of the food
- The list of ingredients, with allergens emphasised
- Net quantity
- Date of minimum durability or use-by date
- Any special storage conditions or conditions of use
- The name and address of the food business operator
- A nutrition declaration
- Instructions for use where needed

For a small jar, that is a lot of text. A 60 × 60 mm label holds roughly 180–220 words at 6–7 pt, which is why EU food labels often use a wrap or a back panel rather than a single front label.

## What this does to label size

| Channel | Typical label | Text load |
|---|---|---|
| DTC | 50 × 70 mm | Brand, variant, volume |
| Marketplace | 60 × 90 mm | Brand, variant, warning, barcode |
| Retail, US | 70 × 100 mm | Above plus net quantity, address, origin |
| Retail, EU food | 80 × 120 mm or a wrap | Above plus ingredients, allergens, nutrition |

The jump from DTC to retail is usually 50–100% more area. Designing the retail label first and the DTC label as a subset is cheaper than the reverse.

## Barcode placement

Two rules that save arguments with a retailer:

1. **Keep the barcode clear of the fold, seam and curve.** A symbol across a fold will not scan.
2. **Leave the quiet zone.** Roughly 3 mm each side at 100% magnification; a border or a background pattern inside it kills the symbol.

Retailers publish their own vendor guides — barcode height, placement, minimum size — and they differ. Ask for the guide at onboarding rather than assuming the last retailer's rules apply.

## Retailer-specific requirements

Beyond the legal baseline, most retailers add their own:

- A minimum barcode size and a required grade.
- Placement rules (lower right quadrant, a set distance from the base).
- Shelf-ready packaging marks.
- Case and pallet label specifications.
- Their own item number alongside the GTIN.

None of these are negotiable, and none are in the law. They live in the vendor guide, which is why the first step of a retail launch is asking for it.

## Common rejections

| Problem | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Net quantity missing or in one unit system only | FPLA requires both metric and US customary |
| Business address is a PO box or a brand name only | Must be a physical address of the responsible business |
| Country of origin missing | Customs and retail both check |
| Allergens not emphasised | EU requirement; bold or caps, not just listed |
| Barcode too small or in a fold | Will not scan at the till |
| Label smaller than the text requires | Text drops below legible size and the label is rejected |

## What this means for buyers

**Design the retail label first.** It is the superset, and the DTC version is a subset of it.

**Get the retailer's vendor guide before designing.** The legal minimum and the retailer's requirement are different documents.

**Count the words before choosing a size.** A 60 mm label holds about 200 words; nutrition and ingredients can exceed that alone.

**Keep the address and origin on every version.** They are the items most often dropped when a label is adapted.

**Check the barcode after lamination.** Gloss film changes reflection, and a grade measured before lamination is not the grade at the till.
