A chemical label has a different job from a retail brand label. It has to tell a worker or downstream user what the material is, how it can harm them and what to do about it before handling begins. The visual hierarchy is deliberately rigid: hazard communication comes first, and the supplier's marketing does not get to displace it.

This is general production information, not legal advice. Classification and label approval belong with the supplier and its qualified advisers; read the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/). Part of the [label compliance guide](/knowledge/label-compliance/).

## The label is a hazard communication

Under the Globally Harmonized System, or GHS, a chemical label is built from a classification. The classifier decides whether the substance or mixture is, for example, flammable, corrosive, acutely toxic or a health hazard, and the label then carries the corresponding signal word, hazard statements, pictograms and precautionary statements.

That means a printer cannot improve the label by choosing a more familiar icon or a shorter hazard phrase. The elements are outputs of the classification and the market's adopted rule. If the classification changes, the label changes with it. The artwork should carry a version tied to the safety data sheet and the formula revision.

## US: OSHA HazCom 1910.1200

OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard is in 29 CFR 1910.1200. For a supplier label, the required elements include the product identifier, signal word, hazard statements, pictograms, precautionary statements and the name, address and telephone number of the manufacturer, importer or other responsible party.

The standard also addresses workplace labelling for containers in use and alternatives where a label is not feasible. Those provisions are not a general licence for a supplier to ship an unlabelled container. The supplier should confirm which label type applies to the package and the downstream use. OSHA's [HazCom page](https://www.osha.gov/hazcom) and the current standard are the source to check.

## EU: CLP Regulation 1272/2008

In the EU, Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008, known as CLP, sets the classification, labelling and packaging rules. A CLP label includes supplier details, product identifiers, hazard pictograms, a signal word, hazard statements and precautionary statements, plus any supplemental EU hazard statements that apply.

For certain mixtures, CLP also requires a unique formula identifier, or UFI, on the label. The UFI is a product-identification code used in poison-centre notification, not a replacement for the batch number or the supplier's address. The current regulation and its exceptions determine whether the mixture needs one.

## US and EU label elements side by side

| Element | United States, OSHA HazCom | European Union, CLP |
|---|---|---|
| Product identifier | Required | Required |
| Supplier or responsible party | Name, address and telephone number of the manufacturer, importer or responsible party | Supplier details as required by CLP |
| Signal word | “Danger” or “Warning” where classified | “Danger” or “Warning” where classified |
| Hazard statements | Required for the classification | Required for the classification, with EU supplemental statements where applicable |
| Pictograms | Required GHS hazard pictograms for the classification | Required CLP hazard pictograms for the classification |
| Precautionary statements | Required | Required |
| Unique formula identifier | Not an OSHA HazCom label element | Required for certain mixtures, subject to exceptions |
| Environmental pictogram | Not required for OSHA HazCom workplace scope | Can be required for environmental hazards under CLP |
| Nominal quantity | Not a GHS label element; packaging and transport rules can apply | Required by CLP unless a specific rule says otherwise |

## Pictograms: what the symbols do and do not replace

The nine GHS pictograms cover physical, health and environmental hazards: exploding bomb, flame, flame over circle, gas cylinder, corrosion, skull and crossbones, exclamation mark, health hazard and environment. A label uses only the pictograms that match the classification, subject to precedence rules where several hazards apply.

A pictogram is not a complete warning and is not interchangeable with another symbol. The flame over circle means an oxidiser, not a generic fire risk; the exclamation mark covers a set of lower-severity hazards, not every irritation. The printer should reproduce the supplied symbol artwork at the approved size and contrast, not redraw it from a stock icon.

## Small containers and multi-language layouts

Small containers create a physical conflict between hazard information and available area. A 100 ml bottle may need several hazard statements, precautionary statements, supplier details and a UFI in addition to the product identifier. A fold-out label, a tag or a reduced panel may be possible in some markets, but the route is specific and often conditional.

Multi-language packs multiply the block. Start with the longest required language and lay out the full hazard set before choosing the label shape. If the text does not fit, do not remove the signal word or a hazard statement to make room. Change the construction or confirm a permitted small-package route with the authority.

## Label durability on chemical containers

Chemical containers are often HDPE or PP, and they may be exposed to the contents, splashes, condensation, cleaning, sunlight and abrasion in storage. The face stock, adhesive and print must be considered together. A material that survives water is not automatically resistant to a solvent, an acid or a surfactant, and a strong adhesive can still fail if the container surface is contaminated.

Ask for a construction recommendation for the actual chemistry, then test it. The useful test uses the real container, the real filling process and the expected exposure. Include a control sample and record the result. Do not rely on a generic “chemical resistant” claim or a data sheet for a different chemical as proof of compatibility.

The application method is part of the specification. A hand-applied label on a dusty drum and a machine-applied label on a dry bottle are different jobs even when the artwork is identical. Tell the printer how the surface is cleaned, what temperature the container reaches and whether the label is applied before or after filling. Those details can change the adhesive recommendation more than the face stock name does.

| Container and use | What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| HDPE bottle with a solvent-based product | Face stock, adhesive and print resistance to the product and its vapour | The label may lift, wrinkle or lose print even when the adhesive is initially tacky |
| PP container with a curved surface | Minimum bend radius and adhesive wet-out on the curve | A stiff construction can lift at the edge on a tight radius |
| Small bottle with a UFI and several warnings | Whether a fold-out or tag construction is permitted | The full hazard set may not fit on the primary surface |
| Container cleaned or wiped in use | Abrasion and cleaning-agent resistance of the print and laminate | A label that survives filling can fail during routine cleaning |
| Outdoor or unheated storage | UV, moisture and temperature cycling | A paper construction can cockle or delaminate even if the print is protected |

## What the printer can decide, and what it cannot

| Question | Printer can | Supplier or classifier must |
|---|---|---|
| Does the hazard block fit? | Measure the safe area and propose a larger or folded construction | Decide which hazard elements the classification requires |
| Is the pictogram legible? | Reproduce the supplied symbol at the approved size and contrast | Confirm the correct pictogram and precedence |
| Which material survives the chemical? | Compare candidate constructions and prepare a test sample | Confirm the chemistry, exposure and acceptance criteria |
| Is the UFI on the label? | Set the supplied identifier exactly and flag clipping or low contrast | Confirm whether the mixture needs a UFI and supply the correct code |
| Is the language complete? | Lay out the approved translations and show the longest version | Approve the translations and destination languages |

## Artwork handoff for a chemical label

Send the printer the safety data sheet revision, the classification summary, the approved hazard and precautionary statements, the supplier contact block and any UFI. Mark the product identifier, signal word, pictograms and hazard statements as fixed. If the label will be applied in-house, include the application method and the container material rather than only the label dimensions.

For a low-surface-energy or wet container, the choice of adhesive matters as much as the print. The [waterproof label materials guide](/knowledge/waterproof-label-materials/) explains the difference between water resistance and chemical compatibility. The [Proposition 65 article](/knowledge/prop-65-warning-labels/) covers a separate California warning regime; do not merge its wording into a GHS label without a deliberate review.

## Questions to settle before print

1. Which classification and SDS revision govern this label?
2. Which pictograms, signal word, hazard statements and precautionary statements are required?
3. Does the EU label need a UFI or any supplemental statement?
4. Which container, chemistry and cleaning exposure will the label see?
5. Which small-package, workplace or multilingual route, if any, has been approved?

The answers should be attached to the proof. A chemical label is a controlled document, and the print record should show which version of the hazard information was used.
