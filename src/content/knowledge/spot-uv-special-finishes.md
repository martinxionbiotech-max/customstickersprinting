Beyond gloss and matte, a handful of finishes change how a label feels and catches light. They are applied after printing, they add a pass, and each has a failure mode worth knowing.

## The options

**Spot UV** — a glossy varnish applied to selected areas over a matte laminate. The contrast between matte and gloss creates a raised-looking highlight.

**Soft-touch laminate** — a velvety film with a rubberised feel. Premium in the hand; marks with oily fingers.

**Textured laminate** — a fine grain (linen, sand) that gives paper-like tactility to a film label.

**Varnish (overall)** — a thin protective coat, cheaper than laminate, less protective.

**Metallised stock** — a metallic film base rather than foil stamping; cheaper than foil for a full metallic look.

## Where each works

| Finish | Best on | Avoid on |
|---|---|---|
| Spot UV | Matte laminated labels, dark backgrounds | Uncoated paper (absorbs) |
| Soft-touch | Premium spirits, cosmetics, rigid boxes | Products handled with wet hands |
| Textured laminate | Craft and natural brands | Small labels (texture reads as noise) |
| Varnish | Short-life indoor labels | Anything wet or handled |
| Metallised stock | Full metallic backgrounds | Fine text over the metal |

## The cost logic

Every finish is an extra pass. Spot UV also needs a screen or plate for the pattern, which is a setup cost similar to a die — it pays at volume, not at 50 units.

Soft-touch and textured laminates are priced per square metre with a small setup; they are more accessible at low volume than spot UV.

Varnish is the cheapest protection and the weakest; it is not a substitute for laminate on a handled product.

## Failure modes

- **Spot UV on uncoated stock** — the varnish sinks in and the contrast disappears.
- **Soft-touch in a bathroom** — fingerprints and water marks become permanent.
- **Textured laminate on a 30 mm label** — the grain is larger than the design detail.
- **Varnish instead of laminate on a chilled product** — water reaches the ink and the label looks damp.

## When spot UV earns its place

Spot UV is a gloss highlight on a matte surface. It works when the design has a clear focal point — a logo, a pattern, a single word — and the contrast between gloss and matte is part of the look. It is a poor choice when the whole label is already glossy, when the highlight is a hairline, or when the quantity is too low to absorb the screen and pass.

| Situation | Spot UV? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Matte label with a bold logo | Yes | The contrast is visible and reinforces the brand |
| Full-bleed photo with no focal point | Usually no | The highlight has nothing to sit on |
| Very small label | Often no | The screen and registration are hard to see |
| 50-unit test order | Usually no | The setup dominates the price |
| Premium carton or hang tag | Yes, at volume | The tactile contrast reads as quality |

Registration matters: spot UV must align with the printed area, and a small misregistration is visible on a fine logo. Ask for a physical sample on the actual stock. On uncoated or textured card, the varnish sinks in and the gloss is less pronounced; on a coated or laminated surface, it sits on top and reads more sharply.

If the budget is limited, a matte laminate plus a well-printed design usually looks better than a low-quantity spot UV with a weak highlight.

## What this means for buyers

**Choose the finish for the environment first, the look second.** Soft-touch is beautiful and unsuitable for anything handled wet.

**Spot UV is a volume technique.** At low quantities the setup dominates; consider a matte/gloss contrast in the print instead.

**Ask what the finish sits on.** A laminate under spot UV is part of the specification, not an optional extra.

**Request a physical proof.** Finish is the one property a PDF cannot show at all.

## When the spot gloss disappears into the label beneath it

Spot UV can be present exactly where the file asks and still be invisible in the hand. On an already glossy laminate there is little contrast to reveal; on uncoated board the varnish sinks into the fibres. A one-millimetre logo is too small to catch the eye, and a low-build screen may read as a faint sheen rather than a distinct highlight. The buyer sees a missing effect where the supplier sees a completed pass.

Base surface, highlight shape and quantity all affect whether the effect reads. A matte laminate under a broad logo is the clearest use; a textured or dark design may swallow the contrast. Registration becomes more visible on fine type, and the cost logic changes when a screen has to be made for a short run. If the goal is protection rather than a visible gloss, a laminate is the simpler specification.

A physical sample on the exact stock and laminate should be viewed both straight on and at a low angle. Confirm the highlight layer, the base finish and the registration allowance in the file. Rub and flex the sample because a finish that looks right can crack or scuff. | Why the highlight is weak | Physical cause | Sample observation |
|---|---|---|
| The base is already glossy | No matte-to-gloss contrast exists | Tilt the sheet toward a light |
| Uncoated board absorbs the coating | Varnish sinks into the fibres | Compare coated and uncoated strips |
| The logo is a hairline | Too little area catches the light | View at normal reading distance |
| A dark full bleed surrounds the mark | The highlight has no clean edge | Check the boundary under raking light |
| The screen deposit is low | The finish reads as overall sheen | Ask for a heavier build on the sample |
