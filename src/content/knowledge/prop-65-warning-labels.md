Proposition 65 is a California warning law with a long reach into national product ranges. A product sold in California may need a warning even when it is made elsewhere and sold mainly through another channel. The warning can be a small back-panel line or a prominent front block, and the choice affects both artwork and evidence.

This is general production information, not legal advice. Exposure and warning decisions belong with the brand and its qualified advisers; read the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/). Part of the [label compliance guide](/knowledge/label-compliance/).

## What Proposition 65 requires

The California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, or OEHHA, publishes the Proposition 65 chemical list and the warning regulations. In broad terms, the law requires a clear and reasonable warning before a person is knowingly and intentionally exposed to a listed chemical, subject to the detailed rules and exemptions.

The warning is not a general product-safety rating. It addresses a specific chemical exposure and, depending on the format, a health endpoint. That is why a label cannot be copied from a similar product without checking the chemical, route and population. The official OEHHA [Proposition 65 pages](https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65) are the starting point, not a substitute for product-specific advice.

## Who decides whether a warning applies

The brand, importer, distributor or manufacturer may all have a role, depending on the product and relationship. The practical question is who has the information to assess the exposure and the authority to change the formula or packaging. If that is unclear, the label is papering over an unresolved responsibility.

For a private-label product, put the decision in the supply agreement: who owns the exposure assessment, supplies the formula and material data, approves the warning text and updates the label after a formula change. A retailer asking for a warning is not a technical conclusion that it is required.

## What “clear and reasonable” means in layout terms

A warning should effectively reach the person exposed. In production terms, consider where it sits, whether it is visible before purchase or use, how large it is, whether the background has enough contrast and whether the method of sale hides it. Correct wording cannot rescue a warning buried under a fold or lost in the finish.

The exact content and method depend on the route and product type. OEHHA publishes safe-harbour warnings and guidance for consumer products, food, drugs, devices, cosmetics and environmental exposures. Choose the route, then give the printer a fixed text block with the required symbol, if any.

## Long-form warnings

A long-form warning identifies the listed chemical or chemicals and the health endpoint, such as cancer or reproductive harm, and may state how exposure occurs. It can require a block of text that is difficult to fit on a small label. The advantage is that the warning carries more of the specific information on the pack itself.

Long form is not automatically the most protective choice. It is one format with its own content and display rules. For a tiny tube or multilingual carton, compare it with permitted alternatives before proof stage reveals that the text will not fit.

## Short-form warnings

The short-form warning condenses the message and directs the reader to the state website for more information. It can be useful where the label area is tight, but it is not a free pass to reduce the warning to a logo. Its permitted wording, hazard description and symbol depend on the current regulation and the product type.

The OEHHA [short-form guidance](https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/general-info/proposition-65-short-form-warning) should be checked at artwork approval. A short warning copied from a supplement, tool or cosmetic may not be the correct route. If the product has more than one endpoint, the wording must still convey the applicable hazard.

## Short form and long form compared

| Question | Long form | Short form |
|---|---|---|
| How much chemical information appears on pack? | Names the listed chemical or chemicals and the applicable endpoint | Uses the condensed hazard message and directs the reader to the state website |
| Space demand | Usually higher; can dominate a small back panel | Lower, but still needs a readable block and the required elements |
| Product fit | May suit a larger carton or a label with room for several statements | May suit a small package where the long form cannot be legible |
| Approval check | Confirm chemical, endpoint and exposure wording with OEHHA or counsel | Confirm the product qualifies and the current short-form content is used |
| Version control | Each formula or material change can change the content | Still requires the same exposure decision before the shorter text is chosen |

## Label space and the translation problem

The warning competes with ingredients, allergens, net quantity, the responsible party and retailer data. A multilingual pack may need the warning in more than one language or with translated material, depending on the market and method. That can double the space even when the English sentence is short.

Lay out the warning at its approved size in every required language, then add the other mandatory fields. Do not begin with brand artwork and shrink the warning to fit. If the result falls below the agreed legibility threshold, change the label size, use a folded construction or add a separate applied panel rather than accepting tiny type.

## When a warning is not the right answer

A warning is a communication tool, not a substitute for designing out an exposure. Reformulating, changing a material, improving a process or establishing that the exposure does not require a warning may be better than a statement that follows the product for years.

The decision needs a technical basis. A supplier's verbal assurance, an old report for a different formula or a competitor's label is not enough. Record the assessment, assumptions, samples or materials covered and the date. If the formula changes, review whether the conclusion still holds.

| Decision route | What it produces | What the brand should record |
|---|---|---|
| Warn with the applicable form | A warning block for the label and any required website reference | Chemical, endpoint, route, product scope, approval date and label version |
| Reformulate or substitute | A product or process change intended to remove the exposure | Formula or material change, technical basis and confirmation that the change was implemented |
| Test and assess | A product-specific exposure conclusion | Test scope, laboratory or method, sample identity and limitations |
| Conclude no warning is required | A decision not to place a warning on the pack | The basis for the conclusion, the reviewer and the review date |

## Retailer and importer pressure

Retailers and importers may ask for a warning as a condition of listing. That pressure is not a substitute for the brand's analysis. A blanket warning can be inaccurate, confuse consumers and create version-control problems when the product moves to another channel.

Ask the requester to identify the chemical, route and basis for the request. If the answer is a generic policy, escalate it to the party responsible for the product. Keep the correspondence, exposure decision, approval date and label version together in the product record. The printer can reproduce the approved block; it should not arbitrate the commercial dispute behind it.

## What the printer can do, and what it cannot

| Question | Printer can | Brand or adviser must |
|---|---|---|
| Does the warning fit? | Measure the block at the approved size and flag overflow | Decide the required language, format and minimum legibility |
| Is it visible? | Check contrast, placement and whether a fold or finish obscures it | Decide where the warning must appear for the product and channel |
| Is the symbol correct? | Reproduce the supplied symbol and check that it is not clipped | Confirm whether a symbol is required and which version applies |
| Is the website current? | Set the supplied URL without altering it | Verify the official warning URL and the applicable warning route |
| Is the version right? | Match the printed file to the approved proof and version record | Record the exposure decision, approval date and product scope |

## Questions to settle before print

1. Which listed chemical and exposure route are relevant to this product?
2. Is a warning required, and which OEHHA route or safe-harbour format applies?
3. Is long form, short form or another permitted method appropriate for the package?
4. Which languages and which surfaces must carry the warning?
5. Who approved the decision, and which artwork version is approved?

The [GHS and CLP article](/knowledge/ghs-chemical-labels/) covers a different warning system with its own symbols and classification logic. Do not merge the two formats just because both use the word warning. Each has its own source, scope and approval route.
