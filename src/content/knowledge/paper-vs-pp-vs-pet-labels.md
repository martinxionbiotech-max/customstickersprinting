Every label quote starts with a material question, and most buyers answer it with a preference rather than a condition. That is backwards: the material is decided by what the label has to survive, not by what looks good in a swatch.

Three materials cover the large majority of products: paper, PP and PET.

## The short version

| Material | Survives | Fails at | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coated paper | Dry storage, indoor retail, cartons | Condensation, oil, long humidity | Lowest |
| PP (polypropylene) | Water, chill, squeeze, bathroom | Solvents, very high heat | Middle |
| PET | Water, oil, some chemicals, abrasion | Sharp folds on small diameters | Highest |

If the product lives in a fridge, a bathroom or a cooler, paper is the wrong answer regardless of price.

## Paper

Paper is a fibre sheet. It prints beautifully, takes a matte or gloss laminate well, and is the cheapest of the three. On a carton, a boxed cosmetic or a dry-goods jar, it is the correct choice and there is no reason to pay more.

It fails predictably when water arrives. The fibres absorb moisture, the sheet expands, and the label cockles — a rippled surface that no amount of adhesive can fix. Oil does the same thing faster: a paper label on an olive oil bottle turns translucent within days.

There is a middle option: **water-resistant paper**, which is coated to slow absorption. It handles a chilled product for a short life, but it is not immersion-grade and will not survive a cooler full of ice.

## PP

Polypropylene is a plastic film. It does not absorb water, it flexes with a squeeze bottle, and it holds its shape on a curved surface. That combination makes it the default for beverages, chilled food, cosmetics and household products.

Two practical notes. First, PP is slightly stiffer than paper, so on very small diameters (under about 30 mm) a thinner grade conforms better. Second, not all PP is the same: white, clear and metallised versions behave differently under the same ink, and clear PP needs a white ink layer behind the design.

## PET

Polyethylene terephthalate is stiffer, clearer and more chemically resistant than PP. It is chosen when the label has to resist oil, alcohol or a cleaning product, or when a premium clarity matters — spirits, fragrance, some cosmetics.

The stiffness is the trade-off. On a small-radius bottle, PET can lift at the edges where PP would follow the curve. On a flat or large-radius surface, it is the better film.

## Matching material to condition

| Product condition | Material | Adhesive note |
|---|---|---|
| Dry goods, carton | Coated paper | Standard permanent |
| Chilled, condensation | PP | Chill-grade acrylic |
| Frozen (−18 °C) | PP or PET | Freezer-grade |
| Oil or vinegar | PP or PET | Oil-resistant |
| Bathroom, cosmetics | PP | Water-resistant acrylic |
| Outdoor sticker | Vinyl | UV-stable laminate |

Note how often the adhesive matters as much as the face stock: four of the six rows change the adhesive, not just the film.

## What this means for buyers

**Do not choose material by price alone.** Paper at half the cost of PP is not cheaper if the product is chilled — it is a reprint waiting to happen, and the reprint costs more than the difference.

**Ask the condition question first.** "Where does this label live?" answers more than "what material do you want?" A supplier who does not ask it is quoting a guess.

**Test on the actual container.** A material data sheet describes a film, not your bottle. A physical sample on your product, stored the way your product is stored, is the only test that predicts the outcome.

**The cheapest correct material is usually PP.** It covers water, chill and handling at a middle price, which is why it is the most common answer for consumer products.
