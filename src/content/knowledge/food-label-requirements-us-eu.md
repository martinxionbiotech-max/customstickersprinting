A food label is a packaging surface with a regulatory field list attached. That order matters. If the pack is designed first, the required fields compete with the logo, photography and marketing copy for the same millimetres. If the field list is established first, the brand can see what space remains and decide whether a larger label, a wrap or a folded insert is needed.

This is general production information, not legal advice. The brand or importer must confirm the final wording, language and market route; see the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/). Part of the [label compliance guide](/knowledge/label-compliance/).

## Begin with the product and the market

Two foods that look similar can fall under different rules. A packaged biscuit, a chilled ready meal, a wine and a food supplement do not share one mandatory panel. The destination also matters: the United States and the European Union use different field structures, different allergen presentation and different minimum type rules.

Write down four facts before designing: the exact food, the package format, the market where the consumer will buy it, and the entity responsible for the product in that market. Then collect the current official requirements for that combination. A label for a US retailer is not automatically a template for an EU retailer, and an export carton is not a substitute for the consumer label.

## United States: the 21 CFR 101 field set

The FDA's food labelling rules sit within 21 CFR Part 101. For many packaged foods, the core fields are:

- A statement of identity that tells the buyer what the food is.
- The net quantity of contents.
- The ingredient list, with the required allergen declaration.
- The name and place of business of the manufacturer, packer or distributor.
- Nutrition Facts where the food and package do not fall within an exemption.

The exact requirement depends on the food and the package. Small businesses, plain coffee and tea, certain single-ingredient foods and other categories can have different obligations. The FDA's [food labelling pages](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods) and the current eCFR text are the starting sources; the brand should confirm any exemption rather than assuming that a smaller pack removes a field.

## Allergens in the US, including sesame

The US major food allergen set includes milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame. Sesame was added following the FASTER Act. The declaration has to identify the food source in the form the current rules require, and it must not be hidden only in a collective term.

Cross-contact statements are not a substitute for an accurate ingredient and allergen declaration. A "may contain" statement may be appropriate in some situations, but the brand should confirm its use with the FDA guidance and its own allergen plan. For the printer, the practical issue is visibility: the allergen statement must survive the background, the finish and the final type size. A subtle line in a dark panel is a layout risk even when the words are correct.

## European Union: Regulation 1169/2011 field set

Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 sets the general food information rules. Its mandatory particulars include the name of the food, the list of ingredients, allergens emphasised in the ingredient list, net quantity, the date of minimum durability or use-by date, special storage or use conditions where relevant, the name and address of the food business operator, origin where required, and a nutrition declaration subject to defined exceptions.

The regulation also addresses the language of the market and the presentation of mandatory particulars. The brand should check product-specific rules and any national measures with the importer. An EU label is not simply a US label with a different address: the ingredient list, date logic, operator details and nutrition presentation can all change the layout.

## US and EU fields side by side

| Field | United States | European Union |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Statement of identity required for many packaged foods | Name of the food required |
| Net quantity | Required, with units and placement rules | Required, subject to the regulation's rules |
| Ingredients | Required, with exceptions and a specific order | Required, with exceptions and allergen emphasis |
| Allergens | Major food allergens, including sesame, declared in the required form | Allergens emphasised within the ingredient list |
| Responsible party | Manufacturer, packer or distributor name and address | Food business operator name and address |
| Date | Not a general field for every food; product-specific rules apply | Date of minimum durability or use-by date where required |
| Storage or use conditions | Where needed for the product | Required where special conditions apply |
| Origin | Product- and market-specific; customs rules may also apply | Required where the regulation applies an origin indication |
| Nutrition | Nutrition Facts where applicable | Nutrition declaration with defined exemptions |
| Language | English generally, subject to exceptions; confirm with FDA | Language of the market, checked with the importer |

## What the EU x-height and language rules do to layout

The EU general rule sets a minimum x-height of 1.2 mm for mandatory particulars, with a 0.9 mm allowance for packages whose largest surface has an area of less than 80 cm². These are layout constraints, not design suggestions. A long ingredient list set at the minimum size can occupy more space than the brand expects.

Language adds another multiplier. A bilingual pack may duplicate the ingredient list, allergen statement, warnings and storage instructions. Translation can also change line breaks: a short English word may become a long compound in another language. Build the layout from the longest approved translation, not the English draft, and keep the source and translation versions together.

## Nutrition information and the space it takes

In the US, a Nutrition Facts panel has a prescribed structure and type hierarchy. In the EU, the nutrition declaration has its own required order and presentation, with exceptions for certain foods. The panel can dominate a small back label, which is why a food label often needs a wrap or a second panel rather than a larger logo.

Do not ask a printer to invent nutrient values or decide whether a serving size is correct. The brand supplies the formula, the tested or calculated values where required, the units and the approved format. The printer's job is to reproduce that block faithfully and warn when it cannot remain legible at the requested dimensions.

## Small packs, folds and multi-panel labels

A small jar or a narrow carton can run out of surface before the mandatory block is complete. A wrap label can carry the brand on the front and the information panel around the side, while a folded leaflet or a multi-page label can add area without changing the container. These are physical solutions to a content problem, not exemptions from the content.

The brand should decide which information must be visible before purchase and which may sit on a rear or secondary panel, subject to the market rules. The printer can show how a fold, seam or bottle curve affects the readable area and whether a barcode would cross it. A two-panel construction may cost more in setup, but it can avoid printing tiny type or using a separate sticker to repair an incomplete panel.

## What the printer can decide, and what it cannot

| Question | Printer can | Brand or importer must |
|---|---|---|
| Will the mandatory block fit? | Measure the safe area, lay out supplied text and flag overflow | Decide which fields are required and which copy can move |
| Is the type readable? | Check the final size, contrast, print method and finish | Approve the wording, language and any required minimum size |
| Are allergens visible? | Preserve the supplied emphasis and flag low-contrast treatment | Confirm the allergen declaration and emphasis format |
| Is the nutrition panel correct? | Reproduce the supplied panel and warn about small type | Supply the values, serving basis and approved format |
| Is the barcode usable? | Preserve quiet zones and verify the printed proof | Assign the correct GTIN and confirm the retail route |
| Is the claim allowed? | Set the claim in the requested size and position | Obtain the substantiation and market approval |

## A practical handoff for a two-market food label

Send the printer a field matrix with one row per required item and one column per market. Mark each item as fixed, market-specific or optional. Attach the approved language files, the exact legal name and address, the nutrition data and any product-specific exemption or authority correspondence. If one pack will serve both markets, show the printer the complete union of fields before choosing the label size.

For a product with a large mandatory block, use the [label size calculator](/tools/label-size-calculator/) to test the available surface, then compare a single large back panel with a wrap or a folded construction. The [cosmetic label guide](/knowledge/cosmetic-label-requirements-us-eu/) shows a similar field-first method for a different product category.

## Five questions to settle before artwork

1. Which entity is responsible for the food in each destination market?
2. Which mandatory fields apply to this exact food and package size?
3. Which language or languages must appear, and which translation is approved?
4. Does the nutrition format or any product-specific date rule change the layout?
5. Who signs off the text, and which version will be printed?

The answers belong in a written record attached to the artwork. Without that record, a late change becomes a guess about which version was approved. With it, the printer can focus on the physical job: fitting the approved block, keeping it legible and reproducing it consistently.
