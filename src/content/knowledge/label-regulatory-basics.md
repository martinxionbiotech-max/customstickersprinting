A label can be printed perfectly and still be wrong for the market. The printer controls the material, the ink and the cut; the brand controls the claim, the ingredient list and the legal text. Confusing those two responsibilities is how a beautiful label reaches a retailer and gets rejected.

This page is not legal advice. It is a map of where the requirements come from and what to settle before the artwork is approved.

## Food labels

In the United States, the Fair Packaging and Labeling Act sets the framework for consumer commodities, and the FDA’s food-labeling rules add requirements such as the nutrition panel, ingredient list and allergen declaration for applicable foods. In the European Union, Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 sets the mandatory particulars: the name of the food, the ingredient list, allergens, net quantity, date of minimum durability or use-by date, storage conditions where relevant, the name and address of the food business operator, and nutrition information with defined exceptions.

The practical consequence for a label is space. A nutrition panel, an ingredient list and an allergen statement can consume most of a small label. If the text does not fit at a readable size, the answer is a larger label, a folded insert or a multi-panel construction — not 4 pt type. A printer can flag that the text is too small; it cannot decide that the text is legally sufficient.

## Cosmetic labels

In the EU, Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 requires a responsible person, an ingredient list using INCI names, nominal content, batch identification, warnings and, where relevant, the period after opening. In the US, FDA cosmetics labeling rules require an ingredient declaration and other information such as the net quantity and the name and address of the manufacturer or distributor.

Cosmetics also sit close to the drug boundary. A claim that the product treats or prevents a disease can change its regulatory classification; that is a brand and regulatory decision, not a print decision. The label may need space for a drug facts panel or a different set of warnings. If the claim is still being debated, do not print the final run.

## Candle and fragrance labels

Candle labels carry safety information: burn instructions, warnings and often a batch code. In the US, the CPSC publishes guidance and voluntary standards for candles and candle accessories; the exact warning text and symbols depend on the standard and the market. The EU has its own general product-safety and labelling rules.

A candle label has a small surface and a large amount of safety text. The common failure is shrinking the warning to fit the design. The better answer is a larger label, a folded warning panel or a separate insert. The brand should confirm the current wording; the printer should confirm that it is legible and inside the safe area.

## What the printer can check

- **Fit:** whether the text block fits the label at the proposed size.
- **Legibility:** whether the smallest type will reproduce on the chosen stock and print method.
- **Safe area:** whether text sits away from the trim, hole or curve.
- **Barcode:** whether the symbol has a quiet zone and enough magnification.
- **Contrast:** whether reverse-out or foil text will remain readable.
- **Version:** whether the file matches the approved proof.

## What the printer cannot decide

- Whether a claim is permitted in the destination market.
- Whether an ingredient list is complete or correctly named.
- Whether a symbol, warning or nutrition format is the current legal version.
- Whether the product is a cosmetic, a drug, a food or a general product.
- Whether the label meets a retailer’s own specification.

Those decisions belong to the brand, its regulatory adviser or the destination retailer. A print supplier that offers to “make it compliant” without knowing the product and the market is offering something it cannot deliver.

## A pre-artwork checklist

1. Name the destination markets and the sales channel.
2. List the mandatory text for each market, with the source and date.
3. Decide the language or languages; allow for text expansion.
4. Measure the available label area before designing.
5. Put the mandatory text and barcode into the layout first.
6. Ask the printer to confirm fit and legibility, not legal approval.
7. Approve a physical proof if the text is small or the stock is textured.
8. Keep the approved proof and the source references with the order.

## What this means for buyers

**The brand owns the claim; the printer owns the print.** Separate the two in the brief, and do not ask a print supplier to sign off on a legal question.

**Space is a compliance issue.** If the mandatory text does not fit at a readable size, change the label format, not the font size.

**Check the current version.** Rules and retailer specifications change; a label approved last year may not be current this year.

**Use official sources.** The NIST FPLA page and the EUR-Lex texts linked below are starting points; the destination retailer’s packaging manual is often stricter than the law.

## The legal line the printer can set but cannot verify

A label can contain a legally required sentence, set cleanly and placed inside the safe area, and still be wrong. The copy may come from an old pack, another market or a formula that has since changed. A printer can confirm that the words fit and remain legible; it cannot know whether the claim is permitted, the ingredient name is current or the product has crossed into a different category. The gap is ownership, not typography.

Destination, sales channel and product classification all shift the copy. A phrase accepted in a DTC listing may be insufficient for retail, and a translation can expand far beyond the space reserved for the English version. A new claim can change whether the product is treated as a cosmetic, a drug or a food. If no one on the brand side can name the source and version of the copy, the label is not ready for a production proof, however complete it looks.

An approved copy manifest should carry the exact wording, market, language, source and responsible reviewer. Place that copy before the design is final, then ask the printer only to flag fit, contrast and version mismatches in the file. Keep the signed proof and manifest with the order.
