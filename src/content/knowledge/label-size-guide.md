A label that is 2 mm too tall creases at the shoulder. One that is 4 mm too narrow leaves a visible strip of container at the seam. Neither problem shows up in a design file — both are decided by three measurements of the physical object.

This guide covers how to take those measurements, what sizes are typical for common containers, and how to calculate a wrap.

## The short answer

Three numbers decide a label size:

1. **Panel width** — the flat area across the container.
2. **Panel height** — how much of the body you want to cover.
3. **Circumference** — only if the label wraps around.

For a front-panel label, use width as measured and about 60–80% of the body height. For a wrap, use the circumference plus 3–5 mm of overlap.

Two things this table does not tell you: where the panel actually is on your container, and whether the surface is flat or curved. Both change the answer, which is why the next two sections matter more than the table.

## How to measure a container you already have

You need a ruler with millimetres and, for wraps, a soft tape or a piece of string.

**1. Find the printable panel.** On a bottle, that is the cylindrical section between the shoulder radius and the base radius. Hold the container at eye level: the area where the surface is vertical and not curving in is the panel.

**2. Measure the width.** Wrap the tape around the container and read the circumference; divide by 2 for a half wrap or by 4 for a quarter. If you are measuring a flat panel — a carton, a pouch — measure edge to edge across the flat area.

**3. Measure the height.** From the bottom of the panel to the bottom of the shoulder. Then subtract 4–6 mm of clearance at each end. A label that reaches into the radius will lift or crease there, no matter how good the adhesive is.

**4. Check the taper.** Look at the container side-on. If the body narrows towards the top, a rectangular label will wrinkle at the top edge. Measure the diameter at both ends of the label area; if they differ by more than about 3 mm, ask for a trapezoid dieline rather than a rectangle.

**5. Note the surface.** Glass, PET, HDPE and powder-coated metal all behave differently, and a recycled plastic can vary from batch to batch. This matters for the adhesive, not the size — but it is worth recording while you have the container in hand.

If you do not have the container yet, ask the supplier for the technical drawing. Bottle catalogues list diameter and height, and most give the "label panel" height separately. A photo of the bottle next to a ruler is usually enough to sanity-check a size before you order.

## Wrapping a curved container: the arithmetic

A wrap label is a rectangle bent around a cylinder, so the width is a circumference, not a panel width.

```
circumference = π × diameter
label width   = circumference + overlap (3–5 mm)
label height  = panel height − 4–6 mm clearance
```

Three worked examples:

| Container | Diameter | Circumference | Label width with 4 mm overlap |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 250 ml lotion bottle | 50 mm | 157 mm | 161 mm |
| 8 oz candle jar | 73 mm | 229 mm | 233 mm |
| 500 ml shampoo bottle | 65 mm | 204 mm | 208 mm |

Two corrections matter more than the arithmetic:

- **The seam needs a gap.** If the artwork is a continuous design, leave a 2–3 mm gap where the two ends meet, or the pattern will appear to jump. If the design is a repeating pattern, size the repeat to divide the circumference exactly.
- **Overlap is not free.** An overlap of 8–10 mm is common on hand-applied wraps and is fine, but it hides that part of the design on the back. Decide whether the overlap sits over a blank area or over artwork before approving the proof.

For a container that is not a true cylinder — oval, square with rounded corners, tapered — the circumference is not π × diameter. Wrap a tape around it and read the number directly. This is the only reliable method for an odd shape.

## Tapered and irregular containers

A straight rectangle on a tapered bottle produces wrinkles at the wider end. There are three ways out, in order of cost:

1. **Shorten the label** so it sits on the straighter part of the body. Free, and usually enough.
2. **Use a trapezoid dieline** that follows the taper. A custom cut, so a die or plotter path is needed, but it removes the wrinkle entirely.
3. **Use a softer film.** A thinner PP conforms better than a stiff PET, at the cost of some durability.

On very small diameters — below about 30 mm — the curvature is aggressive enough that even a thin film wants to lift at the edges. In that range, specify a thin face stock with a high-tack adhesive, and expect to test rather than assume.

## How much of the container should the label cover

There is no rule, but there are conventions that readers recognise:

| Coverage | Effect | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 25–35% | Small, premium, lots of visible product | Spirits, premium cosmetics |
| 40–60% | The standard front panel | Beverage, skincare, sauces |
| 70–90% | Dominant, information-heavy | Food with mandatory text |
| Full wrap, 95–105% | The container becomes the label | Cans, candles, some beverages |

More coverage means more space for mandatory information and less visible product. Less coverage looks more expensive and leaves less room for the ingredients panel, which is where food and cosmetic brands usually run out of space first.

## Choosing between two sizes

When two sizes both fit, decide on these four, in order:

1. **Mandatory information.** If the legal text does not fit at 60 × 60 mm, the size is wrong regardless of how it looks.
2. **Barcode.** An EAN-13 needs about 37 × 26 mm at 100% magnification including the quiet zones. Shrinking below 80% risks a scan failure at retail.
3. **Application method.** Hand application drifts by 1–3 mm; an applicator drifts by less. On a small label, that drift is a larger share of the design.
4. **Look.** Only then.

## Multi-panel labelling: front, back and neck

On wine, spirits and premium beverages the "label" is usually three separate pieces, and each has its own size logic.

| Panel | Typical size | What it carries |
|---|---|---|
| Front | 90 × 120 mm | Brand, variant, vintage, volume |
| Back | 70 × 90 mm | Ingredients, allergen statement, address, barcode |
| Neck | 60 × 25 mm or a small circle | Vintage, varietal, a seal |

Two sizing rules for multi-panel work:

- **Keep a 10–15 mm gap between panels.** Applied by hand, two labels that should touch will overlap on some units and leave a sliver on others.
- **Align the top edges, not the centres.** A back label slightly lower than the front reads as deliberate; one that is higher reads as a mistake.

For a small brand, a single larger front label with the legal text underneath is usually cheaper than three pieces, because each additional SKU is a separate setup and a separate application step.

## Flat surfaces: cartons, pouches and boxes

On a flat panel the arithmetic is simpler — width and height as measured — but the margins matter more, because there is no curve to hide an error.

- **Inset 3–5 mm from every edge.** A label that reaches a fold line will lift along the fold.
- **Avoid the seal area on a pouch.** The heat-seal zone at the top and bottom cannot take adhesive reliably; keep the label 10 mm clear of it.
- **Match the label to the panel, not to the box.** A 100 mm carton face usually takes an 80–90 mm label, because the folds and the glue flap eat the rest.

On corrugated board the surface is textured, which reduces contact area. A standard paper label works; a film label may need a low-surface-energy adhesive to grip the peaks rather than bridge the valleys.

## Round labels

A circle is often the right shape on a jar lid, a tin or a bottle shoulder, and the size is a single number: the diameter.

| Surface | Label diameter |
|---|---|
| 63 mm jar lid | 55–58 mm |
| 70 mm tin lid | 62–65 mm |
| 82 mm jar lid | 72–76 mm |
| Bottle shoulder seal | 25–35 mm |

Leave 3–5 mm of lid visible around the edge. A label that covers the lid entirely looks like a sticker applied by mistake, and on a jar it also hides the lid colour that the brand paid for.

## Tolerance and application drift

Every size carries a tolerance, and ignoring it is how a good design ends up looking crooked.

- **Die cutting** holds about ±0.5 mm on a steel die, ±1 mm on a plotter cut.
- **Hand application** drifts 1–3 mm on a curved surface, more on a large wrap.
- **Applicator application** holds about ±0.5 mm.

The practical consequence: keep text and logos at least 3 mm inside the trim, and 5 mm on anything hand-applied. If a design needs to sit exactly on a seam or a fill line, say so — that is an application specification, not just an artwork one, and it may need an applicator rather than hand placement.

## A worked example: a 250 ml bottle

Say you have a 250 ml PET lotion bottle, 50 mm diameter, 130 mm body height, and you want a front-panel label.

1. **Panel height.** 130 mm body, minus 6 mm at the shoulder and 6 mm at the base radius, leaves about 118 mm of usable height. A label at 60–70% of that is 70–80 mm tall.
2. **Panel width.** Half the circumference is 78 mm. A front label of 50 mm leaves 14 mm of container visible on each side and reads as a premium panel rather than a wrap.
3. **Size chosen.** 50 × 80 mm, in paper or PP depending on whether the product is stored wet.
4. **Artwork setup.** 3 mm bleed, 3 mm safe area, barcode at 100% magnification placed on the back if the front cannot take it.
5. **Order.** 50 units to test adhesion and legibility in the hand, then reorder at 500–2,000 once the design is settled.

That is the whole process. It takes ten minutes with the bottle and a ruler, and it prevents the two failures that cause most reprints: a label that does not fit, and a label that fits but cannot hold the mandatory text.

## Five sizing mistakes

1. **Measuring the diameter instead of the circumference** for a wrap, then wondering why the label does not meet at the back.
2. **Ignoring the shoulder radius** and ordering a label that creases at the top on every unit.
3. **Assuming a "500 ml bottle" is a standard shape.** Two suppliers' 500 ml bottles can differ by 8 mm in diameter.
4. **Forgetting the barcode quiet zone.** The bars fit; the scanner still fails.
5. **Reusing a size from a previous supplier** without checking the new container. Moulds change, and so do the labels that fit them.

## What this means for buyers

**Measure, photograph, then order.** A photo of the container next to a ruler answers more questions than a paragraph of description, and it costs nothing.

**Send the container type, not just the volume.** "250 ml PET bottle, 50 mm diameter, 130 mm body height" gets a correct quote; "250 ml bottle" gets a guess.

**Buy one sample size before committing.** On a 50-unit order the sample is cheap; on a 5,000-unit order it is the only protection against a systematic error.

**Treat the table as a starting point.** It is built from the containers we see most often, not from a standard, because no standard exists. The container in your hand is the specification.
