Approving a design on screen and approving a printed label are different acts. Between them sit three kinds of proof, and knowing which one you have prevents most disputes.

## The three proofs

| Proof | What it shows | What it misses | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital (PDF/screen) | Layout, text, positioning | Colour, material, finish | Free |
| Physical sample (printed) | Colour on real stock, finish, cut | Press-specific variation | Low |
| Press proof (from production run) | Everything on the actual press | Nothing | High |

## Digital proof

Useful for catching typos, wrong dimensions, missing bleed and misplaced elements. It says nothing reliable about colour, and nothing at all about how the label feels or cuts.

Approve it for content; never for colour.

## Physical sample

A short run on the actual material with the actual laminate. This is the proof that answers the questions that matter:

- Does the colour read correctly on this stock?
- Does the label conform to the container?
- Does the adhesive hold on this plastic?
- Is the cut clean at this shape?

For a first order, a physical sample is the sensible minimum. It costs a little and prevents the expensive version of the same conversation after 5,000 labels.

## Press proof

A short run pulled from the production press before the full run. It is the most accurate reference and is usually reserved for colour-critical brand packaging.

If colour matching is the risk, this is the proof that removes it.

## What "approved" means

Once you approve a physical sample, that sample becomes the reference for the run. Keep it.

Two practical points:

- **Approve one thing at a time.** Colour, cut and material are separate approvals; bundling them makes it unclear what was accepted.
- **Note the conditions.** "Approved on the 250 ml amber bottle, applied by hand" is a specification; "approved" alone is not.

## Artwork changes after approval

Any change after a proof — even a text edit — resets the proof. A file that was approved and then edited is a new file, and the printer is entitled to treat it as such.

## A proof hierarchy

“Proof” covers several different things, and they catch different mistakes.

| Proof | What it shows | What it misses |
|---|---|---|
| PDF proof | Layout, text, cut line, colour values | Real colour, stock, finish, adhesion |
| Contract proof | Colour on a calibrated device, often on the actual stock | The production press and the final laminate |
| Press proof | A short run on the production press | Cost and time; still not the full run |
| Physical sample | Stock, print, laminate, die, adhesion on your container | Volume variation and final packing |

For a first order, a PDF proof plus a physical sample is usually the right combination. The PDF catches spelling, barcodes and the cut line; the sample catches the things a screen cannot show — the colour on the actual stock, the edge of the die, the way the label sits on a curved bottle.

Approve the proof in writing and keep a copy. If the job is colour-critical, keep the physical sample as the reference for the next run. A proof approved only on screen is a layout approval, not a colour approval.

## What this means for buyers

**Insist on a physical sample for a first order.** It is the cheapest insurance in the process.

**Approve content on the digital proof, colour on the physical one.** They answer different questions.

**Keep the approved sample.** It is the reference for the reorder and for any dispute.

**Freeze the artwork before the run.** Post-approval edits are the most common cause of a mismatch nobody can explain.

## The proof detail that survives a colour approval

An approval can be complete for layout and incomplete for the product. A screen PDF cannot show colour on the chosen stock, the edge of a die or the way an adhesive behaves on a curved bottle. When buyers describe a job as approved, ask which of those the proof actually covered. A silent gap between layout approval and physical approval is where disputes begin.

The proof needed changes with the risk. Text, barcodes and safe areas can be checked on a digital proof; colour and finish need a physical sample on the production stock, and a colour-critical brand may justify a press proof. Adhesion needs the actual container and the intended storage condition. A reorder can reference the retained sample only if the material, print method and laminate have not changed.

Record the proof type, version, date, stock, laminate, container and application method, then approve layout and physical characteristics separately. Keep a sealed sample with the signed approval. A sample without a record is evidence that cannot be reused; a record without the sample is only a description. | Approval gap | Proof that closes it | Record to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Spelling or barcode error | Digital PDF | Version and approval date |
| Colour on the final stock | Physical sample | Stock and laminate reference |
| Adhesion on the container | Applied sample | Surface and storage condition |
| Die edge and shape | Cut sample | Supplier and die identifier |
| Reorder consistency | Retained reference | Signed standard sample |
