A wine or spirits label is a small printed surface carrying a large identity. The producer wants the bottle to look right in the hand, the importer needs the destination fields, and the retailer needs a readable pack on a shelf or in an ice bucket. A label that works on a dry tasting table can fail in a wet cooler, and a design approved for one market can be the wrong version for the next container.

This page covers production decisions. It is not legal advice. Label approvals, mandatory wording, alcohol content, allergen statements, warnings and market access must be confirmed with the producer, importer, TTB or the relevant authority. Read the [disclaimer](/disclaimer/) before relying on a field list.

## The bottle determines the first decision

Start with the actual bottle, not a flat template. A 750 ml bottle can be a Bordeaux shape, a Burgundy slope, a flint bottle or a tapered spirit bottle. The sidewall, shoulder, heel, punt and seam all change the printable area.

Measure the height of the straight band and the circumference at the centre line of the intended label. Check the shoulder start and base radius. A label a few millimetres too tall may look fine on screen but lift after application.

If a front and back label share the bottle, measure them together. The gap between them affects the visual balance and the curve. The back label does not have to match the front height, but it must sit on a printable surface.

## 750 ml label geometry without a stock-size guess

There is no universal 750 ml label size. A bottle with a shorter straight wall may take a 70 mm-high label where a taller mould takes 90 mm. A full-wrap label needs the circumference plus an overlap allowance, planned rather than hidden under the seam.

Use a paper template on the actual bottle to mark height and width. Check shoulder, heel and taper. If the front label has a die-cut shape, make sure its outer edge follows the printable surface rather than cutting into a curve.

For a back label, place the longest approved text block before choosing the size. Importer details, the government warning, allergen information and market-specific statements can exceed the space left by a logo and a barcode. The [bottle label sizing guide](/knowledge/label-size-for-bottles/) sets out the measurement method in more detail.

## Front and back labels have different jobs

The front label carries identity: brand, product name, vintage or expression, and the visual signal that helps a buyer choose. The back label carries the information the drinker or importer needs: composition or origin details, allergen statements, warnings, importer details and sometimes serving or storage guidance.

That division is practical, not universal. Some markets require fields on a particular panel; some producers use a wrap that carries front and back content on one piece. Decide the architecture before the artwork. A front label with no room for a market warning forces a last-minute sticker or a new print run.

Keep the front and back versions linked. If the product name changes on the front, the back should not retain the old name in a tasting note or importer block. A shared version number makes the relationship visible.

## Paper versus synthetic on condensation

Paper is the traditional choice for many still wines and spirits. It can carry a textured finish, letterpress feel and a dry, tactile surface. It can also cockle, lift or stain when water reaches the edge. A coating may slow the effect, but paper remains moisture-absorbing.

Synthetic faces can resist water better and may conform differently on a curved bottle. They are not automatically immune to condensation: water can still reach the adhesive and the edge, and a stiff film can lift from a tight curve. The bottle surface, adhesive and application method remain part of the system.

Think about the service moment. A bottle on a dry shelf has a different requirement from one plunged into an ice bucket or stored in a damp cellar. A tasting-room sample tested with a damp cloth is not evidence for an ice bucket. Test the real condition on the real bottle.

## Premium finishes and real handling

Foil stamping, embossing, textured paper, spot UV and soft-touch laminates can make a wine label memorable. They also change the surface that must survive condensation, abrasion and handling. An emboss can catch on a divider; a soft-touch finish can show fingerprints; a foil edge can scuff.

Ask for the finish on the intended stock and bottle. Check it in raking light and after a wet wipe. If the bottle will be packed in a cloth bag or gift box, rub the sample against the packing material. A finish that looks premium on an unwrapped bottle can fail in the pack.

Do not let a decorative process decide the mandatory text. Foil and emboss need enough clear area and contrast for the required wording. If the back label is the compliant panel, keep the finish away from the densest text block or discuss how the finish affects legibility.

## TTB fields and COLA control

In the United States, the [Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau](https://www.ttb.gov/labeling/wine-labeling) publishes wine labelling requirements and the COLA process. Common fields can include the brand name, class and type, alcohol content, net contents, the bottler, producer or importer, country of origin where applicable, a sulfite declaration where required and the government warning. Exact requirements vary by product and route; confirm them with TTB and the importer.

The approved label is a controlled version. Keep the TTB-approved artwork, label version and bottle/market assignment together. If the importer block, warning or product description changes, do not assume the existing COLA covers it. The producer or importer must confirm that the approved version matches the market.

## EU wine labelling basics

EU wine labelling sits within the common market organisation for agricultural products, [Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2013/1308/oj/eng), together with applicable [food-information rules](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1169/oj/eng) and delegated acts. Depending on the wine, the label may need regulated terms, alcohol content, lot identification, bottler details, allergen information and net quantity. Language and presentation requirements depend on the market.

The EU is not one uniform market for every phrase. A protected term, origin indication, traditional expression or allergen statement may need confirmation before print. Have the producer and importer check the exact variants; acceptance in one member state does not make a label right for another.

## Export SKUs and market versions

Build the export back label before the domestic version. It usually has the most constrained field list: importer details, destination language, allergen or warning statements and any market-specific requirement. The front can then be adapted where the market allows.

Keep a named version for the tasting room, domestic market and each export destination. Record the bottle, capsule, closure, front and back artwork and approved field set together. If an importer needs a small over-sticker for a trial, treat it as a separate controlled version and check whether the market allows it.

| Decision | Working direction | What to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Bottle and label area | Measure the straight band and circumference on the actual mould | Shoulder, heel, seam, punt and die-cut clearance |
| Paper or synthetic | Match the face and adhesive to the service condition | Condensation, ice bucket, cellar and handling |
| Finish | Test the premium finish on the intended stock | Scuff, fingerprint, wet wipe and packing rub |
| Front/back split | Reserve the mandatory block before the brand layout | Longest market version at final size |
| US label | Use the TTB-approved artwork and current field list | COLA, warning, importer and product version |
| EU label | Confirm regulated terms, allergens and language | Market, producer and importer requirements |
| Export SKU | Build the most constrained market first | Version, destination and pack assignment |

## What to send for a wine or spirits quote

Send the bottle or a matching sample, the proposed front, back and neck label sizes, the quantity, the material, the finish direction and the service condition. Include the market versions and the longest approved text block. If the label carries a TTB approval or market-specific warning, send the approved artwork reference and confirm who owns the final check.

The printer can measure the bottle, recommend a construction and proof it on the actual stock. The producer and importer must confirm the market fields and approvals. For the field comparison behind the brief, read [wine and spirits label requirements](/knowledge/wine-spirits-label-requirements/).
